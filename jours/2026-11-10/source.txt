TITRE : Generative Artificial Intelligence and Maternal Health Outcomes in Pregnant and Postpartum Individuals: A Randomized Clinical Trial.
REVUE : JAMA network open · 2026-11-02
DOI : 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.41062

RÉSUMÉ :
Importance Generative artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots are increasingly used to support maternal health, but randomized evidence is limited. Objective To evaluate whether access to a generative AI maternal health chatbot improved 12-month maternal health outcomes. Design, setting, and participants This 2-group randomized clinical trial enrolled 400 participants from October 2023 to December 2024, with 12-month follow-up completed in December 2025. Participants were recruited online across 49 US states and the District of Columbia. Eligible participants were English-speaking, aged 14 years or older, pregnant or parenting an infant younger than 6 months, and self-identified as members of racial and/or ethnic minoritized groups. Interventions Participants were randomized 1:1 to receive access to the chatbot or an active control consisting of monthly children's books. Main outcomes and measures Primary outcomes were anxiety and depression symptoms at 12 months measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 and Patient Health Questionnaire-9. Secondary outcomes included emergency department visits, pregnancy-related complications, postpartum care checkups, effective contraception use, and sleep duration. Results Among 400 randomized participants, the mean (SD) age was 32.6 (5.1) years; 200 (50%) were parenting an infant at enrollment; and 10 (2%) identified as American Indian or Alaska Native, 80 (20%) Asian, 188 (47%) Black or African American, 57 (14%) multiracial, and 65 (16%) Hispanic or Latino. The AI chatbot was not associated with differences in anxiety scores (adjusted mean difference, 0.10; 95% CI, -0.77 to 0.96) or depression scores (adjusted mean difference, -0.23; 95% CI, -1.05 to 0.59), nor with emergency department visits, postpartum care, contraception use, or sleep duration. Among participants pregnant at enrollment, the chatbot was associated with more self-reported pregnancy-related complications in the secondary 12-month end point analysis, but not in longitudinal analyses. Higher chatbot engagement was associated with longer sleep duration and higher anxiety scores in exploratory within-group analyses. Conclusions and relevance In this randomized clinical trial of pregnant and postpartum individuals from racial and ethnic minoritized groups, access to a generative artificial intelligence maternal health chatbot did not improve maternal anxiety or depression at 12 months compared with an active control. Secondary and exploratory findings should be interpreted cautiously. Trial registration ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06053515.

TEXTE INTÉGRAL :
> Introduction
 The transition from pregnancy to the postpartum period is a vulnerable and often undersupported time. Although pregnancy typically involves frequent clinical contact, formal engagement with the health care system declines after delivery despite ongoing medical, psychological, and informational needs. During this period, individuals must manage infant care, recovery from childbirth, and changes in mental health, often with limited support. These gaps may contribute to postpartum anxiety, depression, and preventable complications. 1 , 2 In the US, these risks are amplified among racially and ethnically minoritized populations because of structural disparities in access to care, quality of care, and broader social determinants of health. 3 , 4 , 5 
 Postpartum care gaps are especially concerning because more than half of pregnancy-related deaths occur during or after delivery, and many are considered preventable with timely recognition and management. 6 Mental health conditions are also common, with approximately 1 in 8 women experiencing clinically significant depressive symptoms during the perinatal period. 7 Yet screening, referral, and continuity of care remain inconsistent, particularly for individuals facing barriers related to insurance coverage, transportation, childcare, language, and mistrust of the health care system. 8 , 9 
 Digital health interventions have been proposed as scalable tools to expand access to health information, support self-management, and improve care navigation. 10 , 11 Generative artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots may be particularly appealing in maternal health because they can provide real-time, conversational, and personalized responses to highly individualized concerns. Unlike static websites or rule-based systems, generative AI tools can adapt to user questions and simulate responsive interaction. However, rigorous evidence of their effectiveness remains limited. Existing studies have largely focused on feasibility, acceptability, or knowledge gains rather than clinical or health care utilization outcomes, and few randomized trials have examined longer-term outcomes or focused on populations disproportionately affected by maternal health disparities. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 
 Digital interventions also may not work uniformly across users. Benefits may depend on sustained or meaningful engagement, while associations between engagement and outcomes may be confounded by underlying need. 15 , 16 Individuals with greater symptom burden may use these tools more frequently, complicating the interpretation of engagement-outcome relationships. Understanding whether effects vary by engagement and by participant context is therefore important for implementation and design.
 We conducted a 12-month randomized clinical trial to evaluate Rosie, a generative AI-powered maternal health chatbot (hereafter referred to as the chatbot) developed using community-engaged methods, among pregnant and postpartum participants from racially and ethnically minoritized groups across the US. The trial focused on participants from racially and ethnically minoritized groups because these populations experience disproportionate maternal morbidity, mortality, and barriers to postpartum and mental health care in the US. We assessed maternal anxiety, depression, health care utilization, pregnancy-related complications, contraception use, and sleep, and we explored whether associations varied by chatbot engagement, marital status, pregnancy or parenting status, and infant caregiving status.
 Methods
 Study Design and Participants
 We conducted a 2-group, parallel-group randomized clinical trial to evaluate a generative AI–powered maternal health chatbot. The trial followed Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials ( CONSORT ) reporting guidelines. 17 The target sample size of 400 participants was selected to provide 80% power for a 2-sided test at an α level of .05 to detect moderate intervention-control differences in maternal mental health and health care utilization outcomes, allowing for up to 30% attrition; details are provided in . Enrollment occurred from October 2023 to December 2024, with 12-month follow-up completed in December 2025. Participants were recruited online through social media, community partnerships, and community events across 49 US states and the District of Columbia.
 Eligible participants were aged 14 years or older, English-speaking, pregnant or parenting an infant younger than 6 months, and self-identified as belonging to a racially and/or ethnically minoritized group. The trial was designed to evaluate an intervention intended to address disparities in maternal health among populations disproportionately affected by poor maternal outcomes. Participants were randomized 1:1 to the chatbot or an active control condition using a pregenerated allocation sequence embedded in Qualtrics. 18 Randomization was stratified by parental status at enrollment and hierarchically defined as parenting an infant younger than 6 months vs pregnant and not parenting such an infant; participants meeting both criteria were classified as parenting for stratification. Enrollment personnel could not access or alter the allocation sequence. Blinding was not feasible. The study was approved by the University of Maryland institutional review board (IRB). Participants provided electronic written informed consent and completed the baseline survey before randomization; the IRB waived parental permission for participants younger than 18 years based on minimal risk and Maryland law.
 Intervention Groups
 Participants assigned to the chatbot received access to a conversational AI chatbot designed to provide evidence-based maternal and infant health information, personalized guidance, reminders, and educational support during pregnancy and post partum (eFigure 1 and eTable 1 in ). The chatbot was developed using community-engaged methods, including feedback from diverse pregnant and postpartum individuals to ensure that content, usability, and informational priorities reflected the needs of the intended user population. However, the chatbot’s responses were generated from a shared evidence-based knowledge base and were not algorithmically tailored according to participants’ race or ethnicity. The chatbot included automated safety alerts for messages containing mental or physical distress, including expressions of sadness or possible self-harm, severe postpartum symptoms, or other urgent maternal health concerns. Alerts were reviewed by a clinical social worker who contacted participants, encouraged urgent medical care when appropriate, and provided crisis or clinical resources. To promote sustained engagement, chatbot users earned $0.50 per question asked, up to a maximum of $45, and the top 20% of active users received an additional incentive, a set of children’s board books. Control participants were enrolled in a monthly children’s book club and received 1 board book per month for 12 months. Both groups also completed study surveys and could communicate with study staff as needed.
 Data Collection and Outcomes
 Participants completed self-administered online surveys at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months and received $15 per survey. All health outcomes were self-reported. Chatbot engagement, including number of questions asked, was captured automatically by the application and merged with survey data.
 Primary outcomes were 12-month anxiety and depression symptoms, measured with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and analyzed continuously, using scores of 10 or greater to indicate moderate to severe symptoms. 19 , 20 The original protocol included the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale and PHQ-9; subsequent IRB-approved amendments removed the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale and added the GAD-7 (). The final analytic plan designated 12-month PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores as primary outcomes because both were consistently administered at the end point. Secondary outcomes included emergency department visits, postpartum care checkups, pregnancy-related complications, effective contraception use, and sleep duration.
 Emergency department visits were self-reported as counts over the prior 12 months. Postpartum care checkups were categorized as completed or scheduled vs not completed. Pregnancy-related complications were self-reported and referred to participants’ most recent (index) pregnancy. Reported complications included conditions such as gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders, preterm labor, and pregnancy loss, and were summed as a count variable. Effective contraception included intrauterine devices, oral contraceptive pills, implants, patch or vaginal ring, injectable contraception, tubal ligation, partner vasectomy, or condoms. Sleep duration was self-reported average nightly hours of sleep.
 Statistical Analysis
 We first compared end point outcomes descriptively between groups using t tests or Wilcoxon rank sum tests for continuous variables and χ 2 or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables. We then estimated intervention effects using linear regression for continuous outcomes, Poisson regression for count outcomes, and modified Poisson regression for binary outcomes. Mean differences were calculated as chatbot group minus control group. Rate ratios and prevalence ratios were calculated with the chatbot group as the numerator and the control group as the reference. For outcomes assessed at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months, we conducted longitudinal analyses using mixed-effects models with fixed effects for intervention group, time, and the time-by-intervention interaction.
 Adjusted models controlled for maternal age (years), self-reported race (American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black or African American, multiracial, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, or White), self-reported ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino or not Hispanic or Latino), education level (less than high school, high school or GED, associate’s degree, bachelor’s degree, master’s degree, or doctoral or professional degree), household income, which was collected using prespecified survey categories (0-$30 000, $30 001-$50 000, $50 001-$75 000, $75 001-$100 000, $100 001-$200 000, $200 001 or greater, or prefer not to say), postpregnancy health insurance type (private, Medicaid, other government plan or program, Indian Health Service or Tribal Health Care, other, or no insurance). Adjusted models included baseline values of the corresponding outcome when available, including baseline PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores.
 Pregnancy-related complication analyses were restricted to participants pregnant at enrollment because complications reported by postpartum participants could have occurred before randomization. Race and ethnicity were self-reported using investigator-defined categories adapted from federal reporting standards. These variables were assessed because the intervention targeted groups disproportionately affected by maternal health disparities. Participants identifying as Hispanic or Latino were categorized as such; all others were categorized by self-reported race.
 Exploratory analyses tested heterogeneity by parental status, infant caregiving status, and marital status using stratified models and interaction terms. Within-chatbot analyses examined nonrandomized associations between engagement, measured by chatbot question counts, and outcomes. Because counts were right-skewed, engagement was categorized by distribution as no use (3 participants [1.6%]), low use (44 participants [23.1%]), medium use (95 participants [50%]), high use (41 participants [21.6%]), and super use (7 participants [3.7%]); analyses compared medium, high, and super-user with no or low engagement users. These analyses used the primary covariate adjustment strategy and were interpreted cautiously. Prespecified primary outcomes were considered the main trial findings; no formal multiplicity adjustment was applied, and secondary and additional analyses were hypothesis-generating. Data were analyzed using Python version 3.11 (Python Software Foundation). A 2-sided α level of .05 was considered significant.

 Overall Outcomes
 The 12-month outcome distributions were broadly similar between groups (). A total of 90 participants (23%) reported at least 1 emergency department visit over the prior year, 359 (95%) reported completing or scheduling a postpartum care checkup, and 204 (65%) reported use of an effective contraceptive method. Mean (SD) sleep duration was approximately 6.5 (1.4) hours per night (). Mean anxiety and depression scores were low overall, and unadjusted end point comparisons did not differ significantly between groups.
 Primary Outcomes
 The chatbot was not associated with improvements in anxiety or depression at 12 months (). In adjusted models, the mean difference in GAD-7 score comparing the chatbot group with the control group was 0.10 among 361 participants (95% CI, −0.77 to 0.96), and the mean difference in PHQ-9 score was −0.23 among 362 participants (95% CI, −1.05 to 0.59). Similarly, the chatbot was not associated with lower prevalence of moderate to severe anxiety (adjusted prevalence ratio [aPR], 1.06; 95% CI, 0.61 to 1.83) or moderate to severe depression (aPR, 1.28; 95% CI, 0.70 to 2.36).

 Longitudinal findings were consistent with the primary analyses based on the 12-month outcomes.  Exploratory Subgroup and Within-Group Analyses
 Among participants assigned to the chatbot, 187 (98.4%) asked at least 1 question during the trial. The median (IQR) number of questions asked was 119 (47.2-222.2), and the mean (SD) number was 163.4 (181.1). Among chatbot users, greater engagement was associated with longer sleep duration (adjusted difference, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.01-0.99 hours) but also with higher anxiety scores (adjusted difference, 1.63; 95% CI, 0.18-3.09 points on the GAD-7) (). Greater engagement was not significantly associated with depression, postpartum care, pregnancy complications, or effective contraception after adjustment.

 Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested heterogeneity by marital status and infant caregiving status. Pregnancy vs parenting status at enrollment did not meaningfully modify intervention associations across outcomes. However, among never married or separated participants, the chatbot was associated with higher rates of emergency department visits than the book club control (adjusted rate ratio [aRR], 3.22; 95% CI, 1.36-7.61), whereas no significant association was observed among married or cohabiting participants. The chatbot was also associated with higher rates of pregnancy complications among never married or separated participants who were pregnant at enrollment (adjusted rate ratio [aRR], 2.95; 95% CI, 1.35-6.46) (eTable 4 in ). In contrast, among married or cohabiting participants, the chatbot was associated with greater effective contraception use (aPR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.06-1.51), with no significant association among never married or separated participants (eTable 4 in ). Infant caregiving status modified anxiety findings. Among participants parenting infants younger than 7 months, the chatbot was associated with a higher prevalence of moderate to severe anxiety than the control condition (aPR, 4.14; 95% CI, 1.43-11.94) (eTable 5 in ). No such association was observed among those parenting infants aged 7 months or older.
 No intervention-related adverse events were reported in either group. From October 2023 to December 2026, automated safety alerts were triggered 262 times among 97 chatbot participants. Safety alerts were applicable only to chatbot participants because the control group did not have chatbot access. Alerts were reviewed by study staff; when questions or statements indicated mental or physical distress, staff contacted participants, provided referrals or tailored resources, and followed up 1 month later to assess safety and provide additional support as needed.
 Discussion
 In this randomized clinical trial among pregnant and postpartum participants from racially and ethnically minoritized groups, access to the chatbot did not improve anxiety, depression, health care utilization, sleep, or postpartum care over 12 months. The higher rate of self-reported pregnancy-related complications among participants pregnant at baseline was unexpected and should be considered hypothesis-generating; increased symptom awareness, differential reporting, residual imbalance, or chance may explain this finding.
 The null mental health findings suggest that conversational, information-oriented AI support alone may be insufficient to improve perinatal mental health, which is shaped by structural stressors, caregiving burden, financial strain, social support, and access to clinical care. 3 , 4 , 5 , 8 , 9 The active control condition, which included monthly children’s books and optional study-staff contact, may also have attenuated between-group differences while providing a meaningful comparator.
 Exploratory analyses suggested that engagement and context may matter. Greater chatbot engagement was associated with longer sleep but higher anxiety, possibly reflecting reverse causation, whereby more anxious participants used the chatbot more often, or anxiety amplification through repeated symptom-focused information seeking. 12 , 21 , 22 , 23 Subgroup findings also varied by marital and caregiving status, suggesting that maternal AI tools may function differently depending on social support and parenting stage.
 These findings align with literature showing heterogeneous and often modest effects of digital health and conversational agents on perinatal mental health outcomes. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Prior maternal chatbot studies have largely emphasized feasibility, acceptability, knowledge, or engagement rather than randomized clinical or utilization outcomes. 10 , 11 
 Strengths and Limitations
 Strengths of this study include its randomized design, national recruitment, focus on racially and ethnically minoritized participants, assessment of mental health and utilization outcomes, and availability of app engagement data. Limitations should be noted. Outcomes were self-reported and may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. We did not systematically measure concomitant mental health treatment, social services, or other outside supports during follow-up. Differential use of outside care could have influenced mental health and utilization outcomes. Engagement was not randomized, so engagement analyses cannot support causal inference. The study was powered to detect moderate effects, and smaller but potentially meaningful effects may have been missed. Effective contraception analyses included fewer participants than other outcomes because this item was not applicable or not answered by all participants. Consequently, the smaller analytic sample may have reduced statistical precision and may limit interpretation of contraception findings. The active control condition may have attenuated between-group differences because participants in the control group received monthly children’s books and could communicate with study staff. Thus, the trial compared the chatbot with an active control intervention rather than no intervention. In addition, the chatbot incorporated automated safety alerts and, in some cases, human follow-up from research staff for clinically concerning content, which may limit generalizability to fully automated maternal health chatbots.
 Conclusions
 In this randomized clinical trial of pregnant and postpartum participants from racially and ethnically minoritized groups, access to a generative AI maternal health chatbot did not improve the primary outcomes of anxiety or depression at 12 months compared with active control. The intervention was also not associated with improvements in most secondary maternal health outcomes. Secondary and exploratory findings, including the higher adjusted rate of self-reported pregnancy-related complications in one end point analysis and associations with chatbot engagement and participant context, should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating. These findings suggest that generative AI maternal health chatbots may be better positioned as adjunctive tools rather than stand-alone interventions for improving maternal health during pregnancy and post partum.
