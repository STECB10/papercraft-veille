TITRE : Imaging-Based AI for Predicting Pathological Complete Response in Esophageal Cancer After Neoadjuvant Therapy: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.
REVUE : Journal of medical Internet research · 2026-11-11
DOI : 10.2196/98520

RÉSUMÉ :
Background Imaging-based AI may help predict pathological complete response (pCR) after neoadjuvant therapy for esophageal cancer. However, an encouraging average diagnostic performance does not establish reliable performance across clinical settings or justify treatment decisions without further validation. Objective We aimed to assess the predictive performance of imaging-based AI across neoadjuvant strategies, distinguish internal from external validation, and examine limitations affecting interpretation. Methods PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched through August 7, 2026. Eligible English-language studies evaluated imaging-based AI against pathological assessment of pCR. PROBAST+AI (Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool plus Artificial Intelligence) assessed study quality and risk of bias; GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) assessed certainty for sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR). Bivariate random-effects models synthesized validation groups separately. CIs described uncertainty in average performance, whereas 95% prediction intervals (PIs) described true performance in a similar new study. Results Thirty-three studies contributed 27 internal-validation datasets with 1891 patients and 16 external-validation datasets with 777 patients. Ten publications contributed to both groups. The sum was 2668 validation observations; the unique patient count across datasets and publications could not be verified. Internal sensitivity was 0.77 (95% CI 0.72-0.81; PI 0.57-0.89), specificity 0.82 (95% CI 0.78-0.86; PI 0.60-0.93); DOR, 15.49 (95% CI 11.16-21.50); and area under the curve (AUC), 0.86 (95% CI 0.83-0.89). External sensitivity was 0.73 (95% CI 0.61-0.81; PI 0.34-0.93), specificity 0.83 (95% CI 0.73-0.90; PI 0.36-0.98), DOR 12.93 (95% CI 7.25-23.07), and AUC 0.84 (95% CI 0.79-0.89). DOR PIs were 5.40-44.44 and 2.40-69.62, respectively. Thirty-two studies were conducted in China, and 12 had high validation risk of bias. All GRADE-assessed outcomes had very low certainty. Selecting the first eligible imaging model reported in each article instead of the highest-AUC model showed modest decreases in average performance across 16 internal and 10 external datasets; incomplete alternative-model reporting limited interpretation. Single study algorithm estimates were reported descriptively, without ranking algorithms or interpreting their CIs as evidence of between study consistency or generalizability. Conclusions By combining multiple neoadjuvant strategies and separating validation stages, this review extends earlier neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NCRT)-focused syntheses and identifies priorities for further model evaluation. Imaging-based AI may support response assessment, but wide PIs, risk of bias, and very low evidence certainty preclude routine clinical adoption without prospective, geographically diverse validation. Future studies should evaluate prespecified models in independent populations, report clinically relevant decision thresholds, and assess calibration and patient outcomes. Until such evidence is available, these models should be considered investigational adjuncts to response assessment and should not independently guide organ preservation or omission of surgery after neoadjuvant treatment.

TEXTE INTÉGRAL :
> Introduction
 Esophageal cancer is one of the most lethal gastrointestinal malignancies worldwide, causing over 500,000 deaths annually, with an overall 5-year survival rate below 25% []. The main histological subtypes are esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and esophageal adenocarcinoma. Surgery remains the primary treatment, but for locally advanced cases, it is often insufficient for long-term survival. The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recommend preoperative neoadjuvant therapy for patients with locally advanced esophageal cancer [-]. If patients achieve a pathological complete response (pCR) after neoadjuvant therapy, active surveillance or organ-preserving strategies may be considered []. However, pCR rates remain limited, ranging from 43%‐49% in squamous cell carcinoma and 23% in adenocarcinoma []. Early and accurate identification of pCR is critical to guide treatment decisions and promote precision clinical management.
 pCR is defined as the absence of viable tumor cells in both the primary lesion and regional lymph nodes after neoadjuvant therapy. In clinical practice, treatment response is often evaluated using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. However, RECIST 1.1 indicates that primary lesions in hollow organs are often difficult to measure accurately []. Consequently, esophageal cancer lesions are frequently classified as nonmeasurable. After neoadjuvant therapy, tumors may achieve pCR, yet residual soft-tissue densities are often still observed on computed tomography (CT) images, predominantly representing fibrosis or inflammatory changes rather than viable tumor. RECIST 1.1 relies on morphological changes, making it difficult to accurately distinguish cases that have achieved pCR, thereby limiting its predictive performance.
 AI has been successfully applied in medicine, enabling automated recognition and analysis of complex medical data []. Radiomics combines AI and medical imaging to extract quantitative image features using algorithms, potentially reflecting underlying pathophysiology and revealing pathogenesis []. Recent machine learning (ML)-based radiomic analyses have demonstrated improved accuracy in predicting therapy response and assessing prognosis []. Imaging-based AI models show potential for predicting pCR in esophageal cancer following neoadjuvant therapy. For resectable locally advanced esophageal cancer, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NCRT) combined with surgery is the standard treatment []. Recent studies indicate that neoadjuvant immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy (NICT) also shows favorable efficacy and safety in locally advanced ESCC [-]. If patients achieve pCR, they may be considered for organ preservation or active surveillance []. However, differences in imaging modalities, AI algorithms, and treatment regimens result in fluctuating predictive accuracy of AI models, making their translation into clinically useful tools challenging.
 Previous reviews have evaluated the performance of radiomics-based models for predicting pCR after NCRT [,]; however, these reviews were largely confined to this single treatment modality. With the rapid development of novel therapeutic strategies such as NICT and neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT), the predictive value of AI models across different treatment modalities remains to be systematically assessed. Furthermore, existing reviews have seldom distinguished between internal and external validation results, offering limited discussion on model generalizability. Thus, this meta-analysis aims to assess the predictive performance of imaging-based AI models for pCR in esophageal cancer after neoadjuvant therapy across multiple neoadjuvant treatment strategies for the first time, and to separately extract and synthesize results from internal and external validation cohorts to rigorously evaluate model generalizability, as well as factors affecting their performance.
 Methods
 Study Design
 This meta-analysis was conducted in strict accordance with the PRISMA-DTA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Diagnostic Test Accuracy) guidelines []. The PRISMA-DTA checklist is provided in . The PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 for Abstracts checklist is provided in . Search reporting followed the PRISMA-S (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses literature search extension) extension [], and the completed PRISMA-S checklist is provided in . Additionally, the study protocol has been registered in the PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) database (CRD420251207710). Deviations from the registered protocol comprised an update and expansion of the literature search during revision (final search: August 7, 2026) and the additional calculation of prediction intervals (PIs) for pooled sensitivity and specificity.
 Information Sources and Search Strategy
 We conducted a systematic literature search across 4 databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, with literature retrieval completed by 2 independent reviewers (ZL and XG) until August 7, 2026. Our search strategy comprised 3 groups of keywords: AI-related terms (eg, artificial intelligence, machine learning, and deep learning); disease-specific terms (eg, esophageal neoplasms, esophageal adenocarcinoma, and ESCC); and neoadjuvant therapy-related terms (eg, neoadjuvant therapy, NACT, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, and NCRT). A search strategy combining free-text keywords and MeSH was used to ensure accuracy, as detailed in Table S1 in . Initial searches posed no restrictions on language or publication year. As an additional information source, citation searching was performed by screening the reference lists of the included studies and of similar published systematic reviews and meta-analyses on the same topic. No study registries or other online resources were searched, no search filters were used, and the search strategy did not undergo formal peer review.
 Eligibility Criteria
 Inclusion criteria carefully adhered to the PITROS (participants, index test, target conditions, reference standard, outcomes, and settings) framework. Participants (P): The participants in this study are patients with esophageal cancer undergoing neoadjuvant therapies, which include NACT, neoadjuvant immunotherapy plus chemoradiotherapy (NICRT), NCRT, and neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy. Index test (I): This study uses AI techniques, specifically ML and deep learning (DL), to analyze imaging data (CT/magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) to predict the pCR in patients with esophageal cancer. Target condition (T): The positive group is defined as patients with a pathologically confirmed pCR, while the negative group consists of patients who did not achieve a pCR. Reference standard (R): Pathological assessment was used as the reference standard to determine whether patients had achieved pCR. Outcomes (O): The primary outcomes include patient-based analysis of sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), and the area under the curve (AUC). Setting (S): The study setting includes retrospective or prospective data sources, covering public databases or local hospitals.
 Exclusion criteria included studies with titles and abstracts that were clearly unrelated to the research topic, as well as specific types of literature (eg, reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, meta-analyses, and letters to editors). Non-English studies were excluded due to lack of accessibility. This English-only restriction was considered a potential limitation because it may have excluded relevant evidence. Furthermore, research related to tumors at the gastroesophageal junction and studies with overlapping samples were also excluded. The screening process was conducted independently by 2 reviewers (LW and JZ), who first assessed the relevance of titles and abstracts and then evaluated the full texts based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies among reviewers were resolved through discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (FR) determined the final decision.
 Risk of Bias and Certainty Assessment
 The quality of the included studies was rigorously evaluated using the updated 2025 PROBAST+AI tool (Prediction model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool plus Artificial Intelligence) []. This tool provides a comprehensive evaluation of both model development and validation phases, with each phase covering 7 key domains including participants and data sources, predictors, outcome assessment, and analytical methods. Detailed evaluation procedures and results are shown in Tables S2 and S3 in .
 To ensure objectivity and accuracy, 2 reviewers (ZL and JZ) independently assessed the risk of bias in included studies using the PROBAST+AI tool. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, thereby enhancing the reliability and consistency of the final assessments. We used the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) tool to evaluate the certainty of sensitivity, specificity, and DOR, focusing on 5 key domains: risk of bias, indirectness, inconsistency, imprecision, and publication bias []. Downgrading in any domain resulted in reduced overall evidence certainty.
 Data Collection Process and Data Items
 Two reviewers (XG and ZL) independently extracted data from all included studies to minimize bias and enhance the accuracy and reliability of the dataset. Any disagreements between the 2 reviewers were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (LW), who served as an arbitrator to facilitate consensus. Extracted data included the first author, publication year, patient origin country, type of histopathology, study design, type of therapy, reference standard, No. of pCR patients, and analysis approach. Technical information was also recorded, including type of imaging, imaging technical details, data source, type of AI model, data splitting method, AI method, the best-performing AI algorithms, as well as the number of patients in the training, internal validation, and external validation sets, with corresponding counts of true positives (TPs), true negatives (TNs), false positives (FPs), and false negatives (FNs). For studies lacking data required for meta-analysis, we contacted corresponding authors via email to request the missing information.
 Index Test and Outcome Measures
 The primary outcomes included sensitivity, specificity, AUC, and DOR for both internal and external validation sets. Internal validation referred to evaluation within the development data or a held-out subset, whereas external validation referred to evaluation in an independent cohort from a different institution or time period and therefore provided a more direct assessment of generalizability. Sensitivity (TP rate) was used to assess the ability of an AI model to correctly identify pCR cases and was calculated as TP/(TP+ FN). Specificity (TN rate) reflected the probability of correctly identifying non-pCR cases and was calculated as TN/(TN+ FP). The AUC served as a comprehensive measure of the model’s ability to distinguish between positive and negative cases. DOR, calculated as the ratio of the positive likelihood ratio (LR+) to the negative likelihood ratio (LR−), was used to evaluate diagnostic performance. For studies with multiple nonoverlapping patient datasets (eg, internal validation and external validation), each dataset was treated as an independent dataset and included in the analysis. Additionally, for studies evaluating multiple AI models, only the model with the highest AUC from the internal or external validation sets was extracted. Internal and external validation were synthesized separately. Validation sample counts were summed within each group; training samples and repeated cross-validation folds were not added again. The 2 validation groups contributed 2668 validation observations in total (1891+777). This sum was not interpreted as a verified count of unique patients because patient overlap across validation datasets and publications could not be fully assessed from the available study-level reports.
 Synthesis Methods and Statistical Analysis
 A bivariate random-effects model was applied in the meta-analysis to assess the diagnostic performance of AI models for predicting pCR in patients with esophageal cancer. Because sensitivity and specificity were jointly modeled, the bivariate random-effects framework was retained as the primary model rather than splitting the outcomes into separate univariate analyses solely to apply the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) correction; HKSJ is primarily designed for conventional univariate random-effects meta-analysis and is not a general replacement correction for a bivariate diagnostic-accuracy model. AUC was derived from the summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve, and DOR was derived from the difference between the model coefficients for logit sensitivity and logit FP rate; conventional HKSJ was therefore not directly applicable to these derived outcomes. Predictive results were pooled for the internal and external validation cohorts. Forest plots were generated to visually present pooled sensitivity, specificity, and DOR, while an SROC curve displayed the summary point and joint confidence and prediction regions. Z-tests were used to compare pooled sensitivity, specificity, DOR, and AUC values, with statistical significance defined as P &lt;.05. Inconsistency was assessed using outcome-specific I ² values based on Cochran Q statistic. Q was calculated for logit sensitivity, logit specificity, and log DOR. For sensitivity and specificity, 0.5 was added to both event and nonevent counts if either count was zero. For DOR, 0.5 was added to all 4 cells if any cell was zero. I ² estimated the proportion of observed variability attributable to between-study differences rather than sampling error, not the absolute magnitude of heterogeneity. Between-study variance (τ²), its square root (τ), and PIs were obtained from the bivariate model to describe variation on the corresponding effect scales []. Meta-regression analyses considered AI method (ML vs DL), AI model (radiomic and clinical models vs radiomic-only models), and contrast-enhanced imaging (yes vs no), using the categories reported in the supplementary meta-regression tables. Bubble plots were generated to assess temporal trends in AI model AUCs, and radar charts were constructed to depict the distribution of ML and DL algorithms. The clinical utility of the AI model was evaluated using a Fagan nomogram, with a pretest probability of 34%, close to the median pCR rate across 43 internal and external validation datasets (34.8%; IQR 27.7%-40.6%). Although PIs were not specified in our original PROSPERO registration protocol, we additionally calculated marginal 95% PIs for sensitivity, specificity, and DOR in both validation groups to better quantify the distribution of effects across different populations and settings, as recommended by recent methodological guidance on the reporting of PIs [,]. Potential small-study effects and funnel-plot asymmetry were assessed using Deeks test; asymmetry was not interpreted as evidence of publication bias alone [,]. The primary bivariate estimates, PIs, and model-selection sensitivity analysis were obtained using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing; version 4.5.2). AUC CIs for the primary analyses were estimated using the delta method. Secondary analyses used Stata (StataCorp LLC; versions 15.1 and 17.0) with the Midas and Metadta modules. Data visualization used the ggplot2 and tidyverse packages in R.
 Sensitivity analyses excluded outliers identified in bivariate box plots, excluded low-quality studies, and included only ESCC cohorts (). We also compared the highest-AUC model with the first eligible imaging model reported in each article, using the same validation cohorts and pCR definitions (Tables S9 and S10 in ). Models were selected in table order, or in Results order when no eligible performance table was available; comparisons were limited to datasets with usable counts for both selections.

 Results
 Study Selection
 The search strategy yielded 1680 records, including 411 from PubMed, 734 from Embase, 521 from Web of Science, and 14 from the Cochrane Library. After removing 693 duplicates using EndNote 20 (Clarivate), 987 records were screened by title and abstract, and 919 were excluded because they were case reports, letters, reviews, animal experiments, meta-analyses, or clearly irrelevant. Following a detailed full-text review of 68 articles, 35 studies were further excluded for nonimaging-based AI model research (n=8), inaccurate definition of pCR (n=12), gastroesophageal junction cancer with unclear pathology (n=7), unavailable diagnostic data (TP, FP, TN, FN; n=4), or overlapping samples (n=4). In addition, citation searching of the reference lists of similar published meta-analyses (ie, the studies included in those reviews) identified 113 additional records; after screening, 104 were removed as irrelevant, and the remaining 9 full-text articles were excluded because of sample overlap. Ultimately, 33 articles were included in this systematic review [,-]. The literature screening process was systematically documented using a standardized PRISMA flow diagram, as shown in . We identified 17 non-English or mixed-language reports; none met the other eligibility criteria.

 Study Characteristics
 A total of 33 eligible studies [,-] were included. Internal validation was reported in 27 studies comprising 27 datasets and 1891 patients [,-,-,,,,,,,,-], while external validation was reported in 16 studies with 16 datasets involving 777 patients [,-,,-,,,,,,]. Nearly all studies were conducted in China (32/33) [,-,-], with one study [] from Japan, and studies were published between 2019 and 2026. Most included studies were retrospective, using pathological results as the reference standard. CT was the most commonly used imaging modality (30/33, 91%), most commonly with contrast enhancement (28/33, 85%). In both internal and external validation sets, ML was the predominant approach for building AI models. Logistic regression (LR) was the most frequently used algorithm in internal validation sets (9/27, 33%), whereas Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) was the most frequent in external validation sets (4/16, 25%). A detailed summary of the study, patient, and technical characteristics is presented in and Tables S4-S6 in . Ten publications contributed to both validation groups, yielding 43 validation datasets across 33 publications. The 2 validation counts summed to 2668 observations (1891+777); the number of unique patients across datasets and publications could not be verified.

 Risk of Bias and Certainty of Evidence
 The PROBAST+AI quality evaluation tool was applied to assess the risk of bias, with results presented in and Tables S2 and S3 in . For model development, 3.0% (1/33) [] of studies were rated as high risk in the overall judgment of quality, and 3.0% (1/33) [] showed high concern regarding applicability. For model validation, 36.4% (12/33) [,,,,-,,,,,] of studies were assessed as high risk in the overall risk of bias judgment, and 3.0% (1/33) [] demonstrated high concern regarding applicability. Overall, high-risk items were relatively infrequent, with low-risk items predominating, indicating that the quality of the included literature was generally acceptable.

 Discussion
 In this systematic review and meta-analysis, imaging-based AI models showed potentially useful diagnostic discrimination for pCR in patients with esophageal cancer undergoing neoadjuvant therapy, consistent with the biological information that radiomics features may capture. The AUC was numerically higher in internal (AUC=0.86) than in external (AUC=0.84) validation sets, although this difference was not statistically significant; sensitivity, specificity, and DOR were likewise comparable between cohorts. For the pooled sensitivity and specificity estimates, we also calculated PIs to quantify the distribution of effects across different populations and settings: the PIs were 0.57‐0.89 for sensitivity and 0.60‐0.93 for specificity in internal validation, and 0.34‐0.93 for sensitivity and 0.36‐0.98 for specificity in external validation (Figure S1 in ). The CIs quantify uncertainty in the average sensitivity and specificity; the PIs describe the range of true performance expected in a new study with conditions similar to those included. They do not describe individual patient probabilities or establish applicability in unrepresented regions. The wide PIs, high validation risk of bias in 12 of 33 studies [,,,,-,,,,,], and very low certainty for all GRADE-assessed outcomes constrain the clinical interpretation. Although AUC estimates may vary with patient case mix, sample size, image acquisition parameters, and preprocessing workflows, the consistent direction of numerically higher internal-validation performance should be regarded as a possible signal of overfitting or limited generalizability rather than explained away as evidence of stable performance. Comparable sensitivity, specificity, and DOR estimates should therefore not be interpreted as resolving this concern. Statistical significance should likewise not be equated with clinical importance [-].
 Subgroup analyses should be considered exploratory: only a limited number of datasets evaluated algorithms such as multilayer perceptron or Vision-Mamba, and the apparent differences may reflect small-study effects, dataset composition, or sampling variation rather than true algorithmic superiority (; Figure S3 in ). Conventional models such as LR and SVM can perform well in small, structured datasets, whereas newer DL architectures may capture more complex representations but generally require larger and more diverse datasets and may be more vulnerable to overfitting. In the current evidence base, the limited number of studies precludes firm conclusions about the comparative clinical advantages of these algorithms [,].
 For different imaging modalities, no statistically significant differences were observed in sensitivity, specificity, or AUC between CT-based and MRI-based AI models for predicting pCR in esophageal cancer after neoadjuvant therapy. Although MRI-derived radiomics features can theoretically provide more detailed information on variations in tissue composition and tumor heterogeneity [-], the available evidence did not establish a reliable difference between the 2 modalities. This is primarily because the critical imaging signatures of tumor response are reflected in morphological changes, such as tumor volume reduction, and alterations in textural heterogeneity, which can be robustly captured by radiomics features derived from both modalities [,]. In addition, the standardization of radiomic features and the application of advanced learning methods may partly attenuate intrinsic differences between imaging modalities, resulting in comparable diagnostic performance. These findings do not establish equivalence or superior clinical utility of CT-based AI. Since CT remains the standard-of-care modality for staging and surveillance in esophageal cancer, characterized by lower cost, faster acquisition speed, and broader accessibility compared to MRI, routine CT workflows may offer a practical setting for further evaluation, but clinical benefit and cost-effectiveness remain unproven.
 For patients receiving NACT, imaging-based AI models had the numerically highest pooled AUC (0.88, 95% CI 0.77‐0.98) among the treatment subgroups. This finding suggests that AI models might detect critical biological changes in tumors and their microenvironment before and after treatment. For both treatment modalities, NACT and NICT are able to remodel the tumor microenvironment and systemic inflammatory-immune status []. These dynamic changes can be reflected in clinical parameters such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and hepatic function indices, all of which are closely associated with pCR [-].
 In 2021, Kao and Hsu [] reported a pooled AUC of 0.813 for radiomics-based prediction of pCR in esophageal cancer patients undergoing NCRT. Subsequently, Yang et al (2023) [] observed an AUC of 0.84 in NCRT cohorts. Compared with the aforementioned studies, our study demonstrated comparable diagnostic performance, with AUCs of 0.86 in the internal validation set and 0.84 in the external validation set. Compared with the study by Kao and Hsu [], our study evaluates AI model performance across multiple neoadjuvant treatment strategies, extending beyond NCRT, specifically including NICT and NACT. This expansion allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of AI models’ predictive potential across different therapeutic modalities. In contrast to the study by Yang et al (2023) [], we separately extracted and synthesized results from internal and external validation cohorts, clarifying differences between the reported validation settings. In addition, we applied the latest PROBAST+AI tool for methodological quality assessment and the GRADE framework to evaluate the certainty of the evidence, making the limitations of the evidence explicit. To further explore the determinants of diagnostic performance, we conducted subgroup analyses based on imaging modalities (CT and MRI) and AI algorithms, alongside a sensitivity analysis focusing on ESCC. While these analyses provide deeper insights, the interstudy heterogeneity and the predominantly retrospective nature of the available evidence warrant cautious interpretation.
 I ² describes relative inconsistency in study results after allowing for sampling error, not the absolute magnitude of between-study heterogeneity. Because I ² depends on within-study precision, its values should not be used to rank the amount of heterogeneity across outcomes or validation groups. Clinical interpretation instead relied on the PIs, which remained wide, particularly for external validation. A bivariate random-effects model accounted for between-study variation but could not eliminate study-level bias. Meta-regression and bivariate box plots were further conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. The meta-regression indicated that the AI method and the type of AI model were likely the main contributors in the internal validation sets, whereas no statistically significant sources of heterogeneity were identified in the external validation sets. Radiomics extracts high-throughput features (intensity, shape, texture, and wavelets) reflecting tumor phenotype and microenvironment, which are complementary to clinical data [-]. The association between integrated radiomic and clinical models and specificity does not establish a causal reduction in misdiagnosis. These subgroup findings warrant cautious interpretation given the limited number of datasets. Moreover, bivariate box plots identified outliers, potentially contributing to statistical heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses excluding low-quality studies or outliers and restricting to ESCC yielded similar average estimates under those tested conditions; they did not establish stability across new clinical settings.
 Some limitations of the current meta-analysis should be considered when interpreting the results. First, most included studies adopted retrospective designs, which may introduce selection bias, information bias, and unmeasured confounding. Therefore, robust prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and assess applicability in clinical practice. Second, for each study, we extracted only the highest-performing AI algorithm to avoid within-study dependence among multiple candidate models. This selective extraction may produce overly optimistic performance estimates. The comparison using the first eligible imaging model reported in each article showed modest decreases in average performance but did not rule out selection bias. Third, all GRADE-assessed outcomes were of very low certainty, limiting the strength of the conclusions. A first-order limitation was geographic concentration: 32 of 33 studies [,-,-] were conducted in China, with a strong predominance of ESCC. This concentration may reflect regional disease patterns, imaging practices, and publication patterns; the review could not determine their relative contributions. Differences in histological distributions, neoadjuvant treatment, CT and MRI protocols, and feature-extraction pipelines may limit transportability. External validation within this evidence base did not establish international generalizability. The English-only restriction may also have excluded relevant evidence. External validation in different geographic regions and ethnic populations is therefore required before widespread clinical implementation.
 Our meta-analysis suggests that imaging-based AI models may provide useful predictive information across internal and external validation cohorts, but their performance and stability should be interpreted cautiously given the heterogeneity, geographic concentration, selective model extraction, and the very low certainty of the evidence. These models could serve as adjuncts to conventional radiological criteria; however, their clinical utility remains uncertain given the very low certainty of the evidence. The current evidence mainly concerns CT- or MRI-based models, sometimes combined with clinical variables; broader multimodal integration requires further evaluation. Therefore, in the future, integrating clinical imaging, pathology, and real-world data via AI models for multimodal dynamic analysis may improve esophageal cancer treatment response prediction []. Notably, AI remains an auxiliary tool, with clinical treatment decisions ultimately made by physicians according to each patient’s context. Furthermore, technical and regulatory barriers persist, and current AI algorithms still face multiple limitations in clinical practice, including data availability, model explainability, DL model generalization, and clinical integration. Advances in few-shot learning, self-supervised models, and centralized platforms may help build a more consolidated and transparent AI ecosystem, further enhancing clinicians’ decision-making and workflow efficiency [,].
 In conclusion, by combining multiple neoadjuvant strategies and separating validation stages, this review extends earlier NCRT-focused syntheses and identifies priorities for further model evaluation. Imaging-based AI may support response assessment, but wide PIs, risk of bias, and very low evidence certainty preclude routine clinical adoption without prospective, geographically diverse validation.
