TITRE : Artificial Intelligence-generated Clinical Images for Plastic Surgery Education: A Prospective Randomized Double-blind Study.
REVUE : Plastic and reconstructive surgery. Global open · 2026-10-27
DOI : 10.1097/gox.0000000000008221

RÉSUMÉ :
Background High-quality clinical images are essential for plastic surgery education and board examinations, but privacy concerns and limited case diversity constrain their use. The educational validity of generative artificial intelligence (AI) images remains uncertain. Methods In a prospective randomized double-blind study, 47 plastic surgeons (40 residents, 7 senior surgeons) each evaluated 60 randomized AI-generated and real clinical images depicting board-style scenarios. Participants rated realism and educational usefulness (0-10), suitability for replacing real photographs (yes/no), and artificial elements (yes/no). Participant-level means and proportions were compared using paired t tests and Wilcoxon tests. Test-retest reliability at 1 month was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients. Results Realism ratings did not differ significantly between AI-generated and real images (8.46 versus 8.36; P = 0.5044). AI-generated images received higher educational usefulness ratings (9.13 versus 8.75; P = 0.0035). Replacement endorsement did not differ significantly (0.95 versus 0.93; P = 0.4140). Artificial elements were reported less frequently in AI-generated images (0.02 versus 0.15; P 0.8). Conclusions AI-generated images received high realism ratings and slightly higher educational usefulness ratings than real photographs in this cohort. These findings support curated AI imagery as a potentially privacy-preserving educational resource. Diagnostic performance, learning outcomes, and demographic bias require evaluation before widespread curricular or examination use.

> Takeaways

 INTRODUCTION
 High-quality clinical images are central to teaching and assessment in plastic surgery. From early residency to board examinations, such as the European Board of Plastic, Reconstructive and Aesthetic Surgery, trainees are routinely required to recognize a wide range of conditions, formulate differential diagnoses, and propose appropriate management based on photographs alone. Standardized image sets are therefore a key component of objective structured clinical examinations and written board formats. 1 The systematic collection and use of real patient photographs are increasingly limited by ethical, legal, and logistical constraints. 2 , 3 Data protection regulations, heightened attention to patient privacy, and the relatively low incidence of some conditions all reduce the availability of large, diverse, and reproducible image banks. Existing repositories may also be biased with respect to skin type, age, or disease severity, potentially limiting their educational generalizability. Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence (AI) have introduced the possibility of producing photorealistic clinical images on demand. 4 – 7 Modern text-to-image models can simulate cutaneous malignancies, eyelid disorders, soft-tissue defects, and other scenarios relevant to plastic surgery, potentially providing an unlimited supply of standardized teaching material while fully preserving patient anonymity. However, the realism, perceived educational usefulness, and reliability of AI-generated images compared with real patient photographs have not been systematically evaluated in a randomized, double-blind setting. The aim of this study was to compare AI-generated and real clinical images used for teaching in plastic surgery in a prospective randomized double-blind design.
 MATERIALS AND METHODS
 This was a prospective, randomized, double-blind study designed to compare the perceived realism and educational value of AI-generated versus real clinical images used for teaching in plastic surgery. The study was conducted in an academic plastic surgery department in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.
 Clinical photographs used in the real image set were obtained from institutional archives. Written informed consent for educational and research use of clinical photographs was obtained from all patients whose images were included. The image-rating survey among surgeons was anonymous, participation was voluntary, and no identifiable personal data were collected from the raters.
 A total of 47 plastic surgeons took part in the study: 40 plastic surgery residents in years 1–5 of formal training and 7 senior plastic surgeons, all board-certified with at least 5 years of independent clinical practice. Participation in the survey was entirely voluntary. For the purpose of analysis, raters were classified as residents or senior surgeons; no additional demographic information was recorded. The final dataset consisted of 60 clinical images, including 30 AI-generated images and 30 real clinical photographs, representative of typical plastic surgery board-style scenarios. The image set was not prospectively annotated according to perceived gender presentation, ethnicity, or skin type/Fitzpatrick phototype; therefore, no subgroup analyses based on these variables were prespecified or performed.
 Real images were anonymized clinical photographs depicting conditions commonly encountered in plastic and aesthetic surgery teaching and examinations. All images were sourced from institutional clinical archives with prior written patient consent for educational and research use. AI-generated images were designed to mirror the same spectrum of clinical conditions. The 60 images were selected to reflect common board-style scenarios encountered in plastic surgery teaching, including examples from aesthetic surgery, cutaneous oncology, and reconstructive practice. The dataset was intentionally designed around clinical scenarios that can be meaningfully assessed from a single static clinical photograph. We did not attempt highly complex syndromic craniofacial deformities or rare pediatric phenotypes, which may require separate validation frameworks and more specialized image-generation approaches. Using a state-of-the-art generative AI platform accessed via ChatGPT (GPT-5 Thinking), the first (F.S.) and last author (S.L.P.) created text prompts describing patients with typical reconstructive and aesthetic plastic surgery conditions, specifying the anatomical region, key clinical features, and basic contextual elements such as lighting and background. The exact number of prompt iterations was not prospectively recorded; prompts were refined until 2 senior plastic surgeons not participating in the study agreed that the images were clinically plausible, photorealistic, and free of obvious artifacts. No additional large language models (LLMs) or alternative image-generation platforms were tested. A persistent version identifier for the underlying image-generation engine was not prospectively logged. All images were standardized in terms of resolution, aspect ratio, and display size. No labels, arrows, grids, or additional graphic elements were added.
 For each rater, the 60 images (AI-generated and real) were presented in a unique randomized order generated by computer-based random allocation. Each image was identified only by a numeric code on the rating sheet or electronic interface. Surgeons were informed that the set contained both AI-generated and real clinical images but were not told the proportion or any further detail about image origin. They were instructed to evaluate each case independently, based solely on its visual appearance and perceived educational value.
 The study was double-blind: raters were blinded to whether each image was AI-generated or real, and the primary data analyst remained blinded to image origin and rater group (resident versus senior) during data cleaning and initial statistical analysis; unblinding occurred only after the main comparisons were completed. For each of the 60 images, raters completed a standardized 4-item questionnaire (Table ). The items covered were perceived realism (0–10 numeric rating scale), perceived usefulness of the image for teaching purposes (0–10 numeric rating scale), whether the image could replace real clinical photographs in theoretical teaching (yes/no), and whether any clearly artificial or unconvincing elements were noted (yes/no).

 The primary outcome was the participant-level mean realism score (0–10) for AI-generated versus real images. Secondary quantitative outcomes included participant-level mean scores for educational usefulness and, for the 2 binary items, the proportion of “yes” responses across AI-generated and real images. To evaluate test–retest reliability, raters were invited to repeat the full rating procedure after approximately 4 weeks. The same 60 images were presented again in a newly randomized order, using the identical questionnaire. Raters remained blinded to image origin and to their earlier responses. For each rater and image type (AI-generated, real), we calculated the mean realism and educational usefulness scores across images at time 1 (test) and time 2 (retest). Reliability of these participant-level aggregates was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for absolute agreement, using a 2-way mixed-effects model. 1-–3 
 Statistical Analysis
 This feasibility study used a paired within-rater design; enrollment reflected the available resident and senior surgeon cohort during the study period. All analyses were performed on participant-level summaries. For each rater, we computed the mean realism score (0–10) for AI-generated images and for real images; the mean educational usefulness score (0–10) for AI-generated and real images; and for the binary items (“can replace real photographs” and “artificial elements”), the proportion of “yes” responses across AI-generated and real images.
 The primary comparison between AI-generated and real images for realism was performed using a paired t test on participant-level mean scores. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used as a nonparametric sensitivity analysis. Effect sizes were expressed as Cohen d for paired samples (mean within-rater difference divided by the SD of the differences). The same approach (paired t test, Wilcoxon test, and Cohen d ) was applied to educational usefulness and to the proportion of “yes” responses for the 2 binary items. Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed by training level (residents versus senior surgeons). The same comparisons of AI-generated and real images were repeated within each group, using the same paired testing strategy and reporting group-specific effect sizes.
 For test–retest reliability, ICCs with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for participant-level mean realism and usefulness scores for both AI-generated and real images. All tests were 2-sided, and a P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (StataCorp LLC).
 RESULTS
 Forty-seven plastic surgeons (40 residents and 7 senior plastic surgeons) completed the image-rating survey. Each rater evaluated 60 randomized images (a mixture of AI-generated and real photographs) and provided ratings for realism, educational usefulness, and qualitative items for every image (Figs. , ). Perceived realism scores were high for both AI-generated and real images. The mean participant-level realism score was 8.46 for AI-generated images and 8.36 for real images (Table ). The difference was not statistically significant (paired t test, P = 0.5044; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 0.8519). Figure shows substantial overlap between the 2 distributions, and Figure demonstrates close agreement between realism scores assigned to AI-generated and real images at the individual rater level. The distribution of within-rater differences (AI–real) was centered around 0 (Fig. ), confirming the absence of a systematic bias in favor of either image type.

 Educational usefulness scores were also very high. The mean usefulness score was 9.13 for AI-generated images and 8.75 for real images (Table ). AI-generated images were rated as significantly more useful for teaching (paired t test, P = 0.0035; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 0.0043), although the absolute difference in mean scores was modest (0.38 points on a 10-point scale).
 Regarding the binary endpoints, the mean proportion of “yes” responses to the question “Can this type of image replace real clinical photographs for theoretical teaching?” was 0.95 for AI-generated images and 0.93 for real images (paired t test, P = 0.4140; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 0.1421), indicating no meaningful difference between conditions. In contrast, the mean proportion of “yes” responses to “Did you notice any clearly artificial or unconvincing elements?” was 0.02 for AI-generated images versus 0.15 for real images (paired t test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P &lt; 0.0001; Table ), suggesting that obvious artifacts or visually disturbing elements were reported more frequently for real photographs than for AI-generated images. Effect-size analysis showed negligible differences between AI-generated and real images in perceived realism and replacement ratings (paired Cohen d  = 0.10 and 0.12), a small-to-moderate effect for higher educational usefulness of AI-generated images ( d  = 0.45), and a moderate to large reduction in reported artificial or unconvincing elements in AI-generated images ( d = −0.62).
 Subgroup analyses by training level showed a consistent pattern (Tables , ). Among residents (n = 40), mean realism scores were 8.47 for AI-generated images and 8.35 for real images (paired t test, P = 0.4576; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 0.8109), confirming no significant difference in perceived realism. Usefulness scores were 9.14 for AI-generated images and 8.74 for real images, with AI-generated images rated as significantly more useful for teaching (paired t test, P = 0.0082; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 0.0090). The mean proportion of “yes” responses for the replacement item was 0.94 for AI and 0.93 for real images ( P = 0.5532), whereas the proportion of “artificial elements” responses was 0.01 for AI and 0.14 for real images ( P &lt; 0.001). Among senior plastic surgeons (n = 7), mean realism scores were 8.38 for AI-generated images and 8.41 for real images (paired t test, P = 0.9450; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 0.9165). Usefulness scores were 9.09 for AI-generated and 8.80 for real images, with no statistically significant difference (paired t test, P = 0.1708; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 0.1730), but the trend again favored AI-generated images. The mean proportion of “yes” responses for the replacement item was 0.96 for AI-generated and 0.93 for real images ( P = 0.2377). The proportion of “artificial elements” responses was 0.03 for AI-generated versus 0.16 for real images, with borderline significance (paired t test, P = 0.0586; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 0.0431).

 Overall, both residents and senior surgeons judged AI-generated images to be at least as realistic and educationally useful as real photographs, with a consistent tendency to report fewer artificial or unconvincing elements in the AI-generated set.  At the 1-month retest, participant-level mean scores remained broadly consistent with baseline across all aggregated measures: realism decreased slightly from 8.46 to 8.21, usefulness decreased from 9.13 to 8.52, the replacement “yes” proportion increased from 0.85 to 0.90, and the artificial elements “yes” proportion remained unchanged at 0.02 (Table ). The corresponding ICC values were 0.89 for realism, 0.81 for usefulness, 0.90 for replacement “yes” proportion, and 1.00 for artificial elements “yes” proportion, indicating good-to-excellent test–retest reliability and overall temporal stability of the ratings.

 DISCUSSION
 In this prospective randomized double-blind study, we found that AI-generated clinical images were perceived as equally realistic compared with real patient photographs by plastic surgery residents and senior surgeons. Mean realism scores were very high for both conditions (8.46 for AI versus 8.36 for real on a 0–10 scale) with no statistically significant difference. At the same time, AI-generated images were rated as slightly more useful for teaching (9.13 versus 8.75) and elicited fewer reports of artificial or unconvincing elements (2% versus 15% affirmative responses). These findings were consistent across residents and senior plastic surgeons, and ratings showed good-to-excellent test–retest stability at 1 month. Taken together, our data suggest that high-quality AI-generated imagery can match, and in some respects exceed, traditional clinical photography for theoretical teaching in plastic surgery.
 Our results align with the broader literature showing that AI is rapidly becoming integrated into medical education and clinical decision support. In radiology, for example, several authors have highlighted the need to develop AI literacy among residents and to incorporate AI tools into training curricula, both as content and as learning aids. 2 – 5 Similar discussions have emerged in graduate medical education more generally, where AI is viewed as both a challenge and an opportunity for residents and faculty. 6 In plastic surgery, recent work has explored the use of ChatGPT and other LLMs for surgical education, question generation, and self-directed learning, 7 – 12 as well as text-to-image models for preoperative counseling in aesthetic procedures such as lip lift. 8 Other studies have focused on machine-learning applications for clinical decision-making, such as breast implant volume selection, 9 or on AI-based evaluation of treatment results using gender- and age-specific scales. 11 Our study extends this emerging field by focusing specifically on AI-generated clinical images as primary teaching material and by directly comparing them with real photographs in a controlled experimental design.
 To our knowledge, this is the first prospective randomized double-blind study in plastic surgery education in which AI-generated images are systematically compared with real clinical photographs, using board-style scenarios and blinded ratings by residents and senior surgeons. Previous reports have discussed AI conceptually, described use cases, or evaluated learners’ attitudes toward AI tools, 1 – 7 , 10 – 13 but none have quantitatively assessed whether AI-generated images can achieve a level of perceived realism and educational usefulness comparable to real patient images. This distinction is important: for AI-generated imagery to be acceptable in high-stakes contexts such as residency teaching and board examinations, it must not only be technically impressive but also be judged by domain experts as valid and trustworthy representation of real clinical pathology. An important observation in our data is that almost all raters considered AI-generated images suitable to replace real photographs for theoretical teaching, with mean replace “yes” proportions of 0.95 for AI-generated and 0.93 for real images. Rather than perceiving AI-generated images as inferior, surgeons rated them as highly realistic and, if anything, slightly cleaner and less problematic than real photographs, as suggested by the significantly lower frequency of “artificial or unconvincing elements” reported for AI-generated images. One possible explanation is that generative models can produce “idealized” yet plausible representations of disease, without distracting artifacts, background clutter, or suboptimal lighting that often affect real-world clinical imaging. From an educational standpoint, such standardized and noise-free images may facilitate pattern recognition and early conceptual learning, especially for junior residents.
 These results have several implications for plastic surgery residency programs and international board examinations. First, AI-generated images could help build large, standardized banks of high-quality clinical cases for written and Objective Structured Clinical Examination-style examinations. Because such images are not linked to identifiable patients, they offer a powerful solution to the long-standing tension between rich visual teaching materials and strict privacy regulations. They may also address limitations in case diversity; for example, generative models can be prompted to produce lesions across different skin tones, ages, and anatomical variants, improving the representativeness of examination content and helping trainees prepare for global practice.
 Second, AI-generated imagery could be integrated into longitudinal curricula for residents, alongside AI-based text tools such as ChatGPT. LLMs can already generate case vignettes, differential diagnoses, and formative multiple-choice questions tailored to training level. 7 , 10 , 13 Combining these capabilities with realistic AI-generated images enables complete synthetic but clinically plausible cases that can be used for self-directed learning, simulation, and formative assessment. In low-resource settings, where access to diverse clinical material and subspecialty faculty may be limited, such AI-driven resources could complement tele-education and multidisciplinary networks to expand training capacity. 1 
 Our findings also underscore the importance of governance, faculty oversight, and expert curation when deploying AI-generated materials. Although raters in this study reported fewer “artificial” elements in AI-generated images than in real photographs, clinical validity cannot be assumed automatically. Generative models may introduce subtle anatomical inaccuracies, implausible morphology, or dataset-driven biases. For educational use—particularly in high-stakes contexts such as board examinations—AI-generated image banks should be developed within a human-in-the-loop framework, with expert surgical curation, explicit quality criteria, documentation of prompts and workflow, and periodic revalidation to ensure accuracy, fairness, and reproducibility. Our study has some limitations. Although we report the ChatGPT version used (GPT-5 Thinking), a persistent version identifier for the underlying image-generation engine was not prospectively logged within our workflow. Future studies should prospectively archive model/version identifiers and prompts whenever possible. Another limitation is that the study was conducted in a single academic network with a moderate number of participants, and the set of conditions represented in the images, while typical of board examinations, was not exhaustive. We evaluated perceived realism and educational usefulness, not diagnostic accuracy or actual examination performance. It remains to be shown whether extended exposure to AI-generated images translates into improved competence or better scores on standardized assessments. In addition, test–retest reliability was good to excellent; future work should assess natural variability in independent retesting conditions.
 We also did not formally quantify or stratify the image set according to perceived gender presentation, ethnicity, or skin type–Fitzpatrick phototype. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that image ratings may vary across demographic phenotypes. Finally, we focused on still images; dynamic or 3-dimensional representations—such as videos, 3-dimensional models, or mixed-reality environments—may offer additional advantages and will require separate validation.
 Despite these limitations, the present data support a clear message: well-curated AI-generated images can serve as a scalable, privacy-preserving adjunct to real clinical photography for theoretical teaching in plastic surgery. They seem acceptable to both residents and senior surgeons in terms of perceived realism and perceived educational usefulness. However, the impact of such images on learning outcomes, diagnostic accuracy, and examination performance remains to be determined and should be addressed in future studies.
