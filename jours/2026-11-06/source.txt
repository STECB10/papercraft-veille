TITRE : Real-world implementation of AI-assisted PERT-workflow and its association with care processes and budget impact: a retrospective cohort study.
REVUE : European heart journal. Digital health · 2026-10-22
DOI : 10.1093/ehjdh/ztag165

RÉSUMÉ :
Aims Pulmonary embolism (PE) remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality. In intermediate-high and high-risk PE, timely escalation beyond anticoagulation is critical, yet real-world pathways are often delayed. Pulmonary embolism response teams (PERTs) improve decision-making, but activation remains inconsistent. Whether artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled triage strengthens PERT and improves acute PE care has not been elucidated so far. Methods and results We retrospectively screened 2500 patients presenting to our university hospital with a symptomatic central or segmental PE between 08/2022 and 07/2024. Clinical and economic outcomes were compared during a 1-year timeframe before and after implementation of an AI algorithm that analyses CT scans, flags suspected PE, and supports PERT triage. 424 patients met inclusion criteria. Numerical reductions in mortality, hospital/ICU length-of-stay did not reach statistical significance. The exploratory budget impact model suggested potential cost savings, mainly driven by trends in reduced resource utilization; however, these findings require prospective validation using adequately powered clinical endpoints.

TEXTE INTÉGRAL :
> Introduction
 Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a potentially life-threatening cardiovascular emergency, with an estimated 900,000 venous thromboembolism cases annually in Europe and substantial associated mortality. 1 Globally, the incidence of acute PE is rising continuously due to an ageing population and better diagnostics. 2–4 Despite improvements in early mortality over past decades, PE remains a leading cause of cardiovascular death, especially in older patients and those with comorbidities. 5 Patients with intermediate-high or high-risk PE, who represent up to 20–45% of PE cases, are at elevated risk of decompensation and mortality. 6 , 7 Advanced therapies can be lifesaving in this group, but deciding if and when to escalate beyond anticoagulation can be challenging and case-specific. 8 , 9 
 Pulmonary embolism response teams (PERT) have emerged as an organizational mechanism to orchestrate PE care and are currently being recommended by major national and international societies. 9–11 PERT rapidly convene specialists in cardiology, vascular medicine, pulmonology, cardiac surgery, intensive and critical care, haematology, and radiology to assess acute PE cases and coordinate optimal therapy. 12 , 13 
 Amid these developments, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a potential tool to further enhance acute PE care. AI algorithms in radiology can automatically detect PE on CT pulmonary angiograms and alert clinicians and expedite formal diagnosis by a radiologist. Integrating AI algorithms into the diagnostic workflow has shown promising results in expediting care; for instance, a recent study at a tertiary centre demonstrated that adopting an AI-augmented radiology triage system for intracranial haemorrhage reduced inpatient length of stay (LOS) by approximately 2.1 days. 14 Although recent studies did not demonstrate a short-term mortality difference with AI, they repeatedly showed AI-algorithm associated improvement of diagnostic accuracy. 15–17 These advances suggest that AI might synergize with PERT by both accelerating PE diagnosis/risk stratification and prompting the multidisciplinary team to take action faster and more consistently. To which extent clinical integration of an AI-supported PERT workflow could help streamline workflows in a real-world setting improving clinical outcomes and budget impact has not been elucidated so far.
 Methods
 To this aim, we performed a single-centre retrospective cohort study at a single high-volume tertiary academic hospital in Germany, comparing two 1-year periods between 08/2022 and 07/2024: The year prior to AI integration into the PERT process and the year after implementation of the AI-assisted PERT model. The hospital’s PERT, established prior to the study period, is a multidisciplinary team constantly available to advise on PE management.
 The AI system used was a commercially available, CE-marked and FDA-cleared software for PE detection on CT pulmonary angiography (Aidoc, Tel Aviv, Israel). In practice, when a patient’s CT scan showed a pulmonary embolus, the AI automatically flagged the CT scan in real-time and sent an alert to the on-call radiologist and other PERT members, via a secure smartphone application and workstation popup respectively. This alert included the identification of PE, its anatomical location as well as blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, serum lactate, high-sensitive troponin and NT-proBNP as determined by the algorithm. Key time intervals were recorded: For patients who underwent an advanced intervention (such as catheter-directed thrombectomy or local thrombolysis), we defined ‘time to therapy’ as the interval from PE diagnosis to the start of the intervention procedure (e.g. catheter insertion). We collected PERT activation data for each patient. Additionally, baseline characteristics, including age, sex, Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (PESI) score, PE location, and 30-day outcome were recorded. In the AI-PERT cohort, a positive AI alert prompted immediate activation of the PERT by a predefined protocol, in parallel with standard radiologist interpretation. In the prior PERT era, PERT activation relied on clinicians’ recognition of PE severity and manual consultation calls, without AI assistance. All other aspects of patient management were per prevailing protocols and clinician judgment; PERT composition and on-call staffing remained the same across periods.
 Patients were stratified by PESI-score, PE anatomical location (central vs. segmental vs. subsegmental) and by risk class. Intermediate-low and intermediate-high risk PE was defined as those with signs of right ventricular (RV) strain on imaging or elevated cardiac biomarkers, in the absence of systemic hypotension. High-risk PE was defined by sustained hypotension, shock, or the need for pressors. For this analysis, we focused on the subgroup of patients with central or segmental PE, with intermediate-high and high-risk features for which PERT activation would be considered.
 To evaluate the economic impact of the AI-PERT implementation, we performed a post-hoc cost analysis using our clinical outcomes data combined with published cost figures. We specifically examined hospital LOS as a proxy for healthcare utilization costs, since LOS is a major cost driver in acute PE care. 2 Based on internal hospital finance data and literature on PE hospitalization costs, we assumed an average cost of approximately 800€ per patient per day of hospitalization for acute PE. This figure is in line with prior published European PE cost analyses. 2 , 9 , 18 
 AI-related costs were incorporated using the annual total cost of ownership of the AI system and operational costs. All model outputs represent deterministic expected values. Observed per-patient differences were extrapolated to estimate hospital-level budget impact.
 Group comparisons used Student’s t -test for normally distributed continuous variables (e.g. time intervals, LOS) and the Mann–Whitney U test if distributions were not normally distributed. Categorical variables were compared with chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. A two-tailed P -value &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Graph Pad Prism 10 and RStudio.
 Results
 Out of 2500 PE-positive CT scans screened over 2 years, a total of 424 patients with acute central or segmental PE were included in the comparative analysis (181 in the pre-AI PERT era, and 243 in the post-implementation AI-PERT era). The remaining cases were predominantly isolated subsegmental PEs that did not meet criteria for analysis. Baseline characteristics did not differ significantly between the two cohorts. The mean age was 64 ± 15 years, and 45% of the patients were female ( P &gt; 0.05). The median PESI score was 115.5 (IQR ∼90–130) overall, reflecting a population largely in PESI risk classes III-V, with no statistically significant difference between the two study cohorts (114 vs. 117; P &gt; 0.05), indicating comparable initial risk profiles. Similarly, the proportion of patients with central PE (PERT era 39.8% vs. AI-PERT era 44.9%) and segmental PE (60.2% vs. 55.1%) did not differ significantly ( P &gt; 0.05). Diagnostic performance was evaluated in all eligible CT pulmonary angiograms during the post-implementation period with a sensitivity of 93%, a specificity of 96%, a positive predictive value of 99,5% and a negative predictive value of 61,5%.
 Implementation of the AI-assisted alert system led to a significant increase in PERT team activation for eligible PE cases. In the year prior to AI, the PERT was formally activated in only 9.2% of PE cases. After introducing AI, PERT activation increased to 84.5% of PE cases ( P &lt; 0.001), by almost 10-fold ( ). The few eligible patients in that period who did not trigger PERT were largely due to rare circumstances (e.g. cardiac arrest from PE en route to the CT scanner, where the code team intervened emergently, or cases in which the AI algorithm did not prompt an alert due to technical issues). Along with increased PERT activation, we observed changes in treatment patterns for intermediate-high risk PE. Among patients classified as intermediate-high risk the use of advanced, invasive therapies rose in the AI-PERT era. In the pre-AI year, 12.7% of intermediate-high and high-risk patients received an escalated therapy beyond anticoagulation—specifically, 10 patients received systemic thrombolysis, 6 patients received local ultrasound assisted catheter-directed thrombolysis, and 7 patients underwent interventional large-bore thrombectomy. In the post-AI year, 13.6% of intermediate-high and high-risk patients received an escalated therapy, with 10 patients receiving systemic thrombolysis, 9 patients receiving local ultrasound assisted catheter-directed thrombolysis, and 14 patients receiving interventional large-bore thrombectomy. While there was a trend towards increased utilization of interventional treatment strategies, statistical significance was not reached between both cohorts ( P &gt; 0.05) (see , ).

 Time from CT completion to formal radiology report was shortened significantly (1.3 ± 0.5 h vs. 2.1 ± 0.6 h, P &lt; 0.01), consistent with the AI algorithm triaging positive PE scans to be read with priority ( ). For patients who underwent an advanced intervention, the elapsed time from PE diagnosis to initiation of therapy was markedly shortened in the AI-PERT cohort. Before implementation of AI, the mean time from diagnosis to the start of an advanced therapy was 2.158 ± 180 min, approximately 36 h. After AI implementation, the mean time from diagnosis to intervention was 1.133 ± 150 min (approximately 18.9 h), representing roughly a 50% reduction ( P &lt; 0.05; ).
 Taken together, our data show that in the AI-PERT cohort patients experienced more frequent guideline-recommended PERT-guided treatment decision and had much timelier diagnosis and significantly reduced time to treatment initiation.
 We evaluated whether the AI-assisted PERT was associated with shorter hospitalizations and cost savings. The overall hospital LOS for PE patients showed a trend towards reduction with AI-PERT. Among survivors with intermediate-high and high-risk PE, the median LOS in the AI-PERT group was 14 days (IQR 2–19.5) compared to 16 days (IQR 6.75–17) in the pre-AI group ( P &gt; 0.05; ). Similarly, introduction of the AI-PERT algorithm was associated with a reduction of ICU LOS by 1.5 days from 5.5 days (IQR 0–7) to 4 days (IQR 0–4) ( P &gt; 0.05; ). Additionally, we observed a trend towards a reduced 30-day mortality after introduction of the AI-algorithm from 25% to 21% ( P &gt; 0.05; ). To better quantify the system-wide impact, we extrapolated LOS changes to an annual hospital-level scale. Based on the volume of PE admissions and the mean LOS difference observed, we estimate that roughly 354 cumulative hospital days per year were saved after implementing AI-PERT (compared to the prior standard care year), which in turn can be reallocated to other patients.

 Discussion
 In this study, we report that integrating an AI-supported PERT workflow and notification system was associated with improvement the management of acute PE. The AI-enhanced PERT model was associated with an almost ten-fold increase in PERT activations, a near-halving of time to intervention for intermediate-high and high-risk PE cases, and an increase in the use of advanced, potentially life-saving therapies for selected patients. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to demonstrate these process improvements when applying AI in a real-world acute cardiovascular emergency multidisciplinary clinical decision-making setting in a high-volume tertiary care system in Germany. These findings have important implications for both patient outcomes and health system efficiency.
 One of the main impacts of the AI-PERT approach was ensuring that guideline-recommended PERT expertise was applied to the majority of PE patients. PERT was activated in over 84% of cases, essentially making guideline-recommended multidisciplinary evaluation the norm, which improves patient outcomes. 1 Our pre-AI PERT activation rate was low, but not atypical for a new PERT programme. 1 By using AI to trigger PERT involvement nearly universally, we create the conditions for PERT associated outcome improvements to be realized broadly. We also saw a trend towards decreased 30-day mortality, which is in line with current literature when more patients receive timely, coordinated care. 1 , 19 This mortality reduction did not reach statistical significance in the current analysis, however, due to a rather small sample size our trial was underpowered. The observed reduction in time to therapy in our study is likely a key mediator—faster intervention for PE can prevent clinical deterioration and reduce the risk of progression to haemodynamic collapse. 1 , 14 , 20 
 Our data also showed an increased rate of advanced interventional therapy usage in the post-AI era, aligning with more aggressive management of appropriate patients. Notably, this increase was modest (from 11.6% to 14.7% of intermediate-high and high-risk cases) and in line with evolving practice patterns. It suggests that the AI-PERT model helped identify patients who truly needed escalation, rather than resulting in indiscriminate overtreatment.
 We found that the AI-PERT approach was associated with economically advantageous effects. While our measured LOS reduction was modest, we projected that fully realizing the AI-supported PERT workflow efficiency was associated on the order of 2 days of total hospital LOS and 1.5 days of intensive care treatment per patient with intermediate-high and high-risk PE. Our modelled estimate of 900.000€ saved annually in inpatient costs underscores how a large portion of expenditures by streamlining care can be recuperated.
 A study about the socioeconomic burden of PE in Europe highlighted that patients with more severe PE, who suffer from in-hospital complications incur substantially higher costs. 18 , 21 By intervening earlier in those patients, the AI-PERT model likely averts some of those costly complications, for example through fewer patients progressing to cardiogenic shock needing advanced intensive unit care and fewer iatrogenic bleeding events due to a more selective use of thrombolytics. A meta-analysis noted significantly lower major bleeding in PERT-treated patients, despite an increased rate of interventional therapy. 1 , 19 This reinforces that patient safety is not compromised by aggressive care when guided by a multidisciplinary team, but is enhanced because the expert decisions are being made on an individual scale.
 Furthermore, the AI-PERT model was associated with improved safety by reducing treatment delays. This could be facilitated by AI alerts that mobilized the team immediately and by expediting interventions on an individual scale. Our results mirror recent data that showed an AI triage reduced hospital LOS by initiating treatment faster. 14 
 The diagnostic accuracy of machine learning algorithms for PE detection on CT has already been shown in the literature. 22 Our work goes further to show the clinical utility of such algorithms in a real-life hospital setting. It is not enough for an AI-algorithm to detect a PE; with its integration into clinical workflows, it can impact patient care. We integrated the AI into a critical decision point—activating a multidisciplinary team—and detected potentially tangible downstream effects. The AI algorithm should be considered a workflow support tool rather than a standalone diagnostic strategy. Given its modest negative predictive value, a negative AI result cannot reliably exclude PE. Formal radiological interpretation remained mandatory, and treatment decisions continued to rely on radiologist-confirmed diagnosis, clinical risk stratification, and PERT discussion. Thus, AI should be viewed as an adjunctive prioritization tool, not a replacement for radiologist review or clinical judgment.
 Although the time from PE diagnosis to initiation of advanced therapy was significantly reduced after implementation of the AI-supported PERT workflow, the absolute intervals remained relatively prolonged and require careful interpretation. This endpoint captured the entire period from confirmed PE diagnosis to the start of reperfusion therapy, rather than procedural preparation alone. It therefore included multidisciplinary case review, reassessment of clinical trajectory, evaluation of bleeding risk and contraindications, patient stabilization, consent procedures where feasible, and coordination of interventional resources. In particular, many patients undergoing advanced therapy had intermediate-high-risk rather than immediately unstable high-risk PE, for whom escalation beyond anticoagulation is individualized and may follow clinical observation or evidence of insufficient stabilization. In addition, catheter-directed thrombolysis or thrombectomy requires availability of trained operators, catheterization laboratory or angiography suite capacity, intensive care or anaesthesia support, and post-procedural monitoring resources. Thus, while the AI-supported PERT workflow was associated with a significant reduction in time to advanced therapy, residual delays likely reflect real-world clinical decision-making and institutional logistical constraints that are not fully addressed by AI-based detection alone.
 This study has several limitations. It was a single-centre, non-randomized observational study at a tertiary centre, and thus is subject to potential confounders. We attempted to mitigate this by showing that patient baseline characteristics were similar before and after the AI implementation, but unmeasured factors, like changes in staff experience, protocol changes mandating PERT activation and heightened awareness of the team due to the study could have influenced our results. We thus only describe associations rather than causal relationships. Because AI alerts triggered predefined PERT activation pathways, we could not determine the independent contribution of the algorithm itself vs. the organizational changes associated with systematic PERT involvement. The sample size, especially for sub-analyses like mortality, was relatively small; thus, the study may be underpowered to detect certain outcome differences. Adjusted analyses were considered; however, robust multivariable regression, propensity-score methods, inverse probability weighting, or Cox regression could not be performed reliably with the available retrospective dataset. Key potential confounders were not uniformly captured across both study periods, and the limited number of outcome events would have created a high risk of overfitting and unstable estimates. We therefore chose not to present adjusted models that could imply misleading precision. Residual confounding may persist despite similar baseline characteristics, and the results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Several potentially important confounders, including detailed comorbidity profiles, complete biomarker trajectories, contraindications to advanced therapy, bleeding risk, symptom-to-diagnosis intervals, and uniform time-to-event/censoring information, were not consistently captured across both study periods. Therefore, residual confounding may persist despite similar baseline characteristics, and the observed differences should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects of AI-supported PERT implementation. Since our centre had previous experience with PERT and advanced treatment options of critically ill patients with PE, these findings may not generalize to centres without established PERT programmes or access to advanced PE therapies. Because the AI algorithm and predefined automatic PERT activation pathway were implemented simultaneously as part of one integrated clinical workflow, this study cannot determine the independent contribution of the algorithm itself vs. the organizational effect of systematic PERT activation. The intervention should therefore be interpreted as an AI-supported PERT workflow rather than as an isolated AI effect. Future studies using staged implementation designs, particularly in centres without pre-existing PERT infrastructure, may help clarify the relative contribution of PERT implementation itself and subsequent AI-supported automated activation. In routine clinical care, however, separating these components is challenging, because the intended purpose of the AI alert is to trigger timely multidisciplinary review in patients with acute pulmonary embolism.
 Regarding observed effects in mortality, the study observed a merely numerical reduction in mortality that requires confirmation in adequately powered prospective studies. Also, due to the retrospective design of the study, we were only able to perform a 30-day follow-up—future trials are needed to look into long-term follow-ups in this setting. Although we observed a numerical reduction in 30-day mortality after implementation of the AI-supported PERT workflow, this difference did not reach statistical significance. The study was not powered to detect meaningful differences in mortality or other clinical outcome endpoints. Therefore, no conclusion regarding a survival benefit can be drawn from the present data. The mortality findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in adequately powered prospective studies
 There may also have been inclusion bias—our cohort included only patients who underwent CTPA and were diagnosed with PE. It does not capture patients who died before diagnosis or those not scanned due to low suspicion, though that number is presumably balanced between the cohorts. Another limitation is that our cost analysis was modelled rather than directly measured. These estimates, while grounded in evidence, should be interpreted as illustrative and exploratory. Our analysis was not able to provide data regarding cost-effectiveness, but was rather an exploratory hospital-perspective budget-impact model analysis. The true cost-effectiveness would factor in cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained. While we did not calculate QALYs due to the retrospective design of the trial, it is reasonable to speculate that by preventing missed PEs, reducing LOS and reducing PE-related deaths, the AI-PERT approach improves subjective perceived quality of life parameters. Additionally, reducing missed diagnoses could prevent downstream costs associated with untreated PE, for instance, treating a severe post-PE syndrome or chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension. 2 , 18 
 Additionally, our study focused on in-hospital outcomes and processes; we did not track long-term outcomes beyond 30 days. It remains to be studied wo which extent AI-PERT integration has an impact on long-term PE sequelae or quality of life.
 Also, temporal trends in PE management may have contributed to the observed differences independently of AI-supported PERT workflow implementation, including increasing operator experience, evolving guidelines, and broader adoption of catheter-based therapies. We acknowledge the inherent limitations of the retrospective before-and-after design. In the absence of a contemporaneous control group, temporal trends in pulmonary embolism management, increasing operator experience, evolving guideline recommendations, and wider adoption of advanced reperfusion therapies may have contributed to the observed findings independently of AI-supported PERT implementation. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.
 The economic analysis should be interpreted as an exploratory budget impact model rather than as a definitive cost-effectiveness analysis. Most projected cost savings were driven by reductions in overall hospital and ICU length of stay; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance in the clinical cohort. Therefore, the model should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and dependent on assumptions regarding length of stay, treatment severity, and institutional cost structures. The findings suggest that implementation of an AI-supported PERT workflow may have the potential to reduce resource utilization, but this requires confirmation in prospective studies using directly measured costs and adequately powered clinical endpoints.
 Selection bias is another important limitation. The analysis was restricted to patients with PE confirmed by CT pulmonary angiography, because the AI-supported workflow evaluated in this study is activated only after CT image acquisition and algorithm-based image analysis. Consequently, patients who were not imaged, underwent alternative diagnostic pathways, or died before diagnostic confirmation were not captured. This may introduce spectrum and survival bias, as the study population represents patients who survived long enough and were clinically stable enough to undergo CTPA. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all patients with suspected PE, patients with undiagnosed PE, or those with very early fatal presentations.
 Finally, as the study was conducted at a high-volume tertiary centre with an established multidisciplinary PERT programme and advanced interventional capabilities, these findings may not be applicable to other institutions.
 In conclusion, an AI-enhanced PERT protocol represents a promising advancement in acute PE management. While our study showed that the statistically significant improvements were limited to process measures rather than patient-centred clinical outcomes, it presents an important first step in showing, how technology helps to orchestrate a multidisciplinary team approach in time sensitive scenarios. This was associated with better patient outcomes and a better care delivery. As hospitals face growing PE caseloads, AI-augmented systems could become an important component of standard care. Further studies, including prospective trials and health-economic evaluations, should be pursued to prospectively validate these results across diverse healthcare environments in multi-centre trials to further strengthen the evidence base for widespread implementation of AI-PERT models in PE and other time-critical cardiovascular conditions.
