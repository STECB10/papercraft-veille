TITRE : Developing and Externally Validating Machine Learning Models to Forecast Short-Term Risk of Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia.
REVUE : Critical care explorations · 2026-11-24
DOI : 10.1097/cce.0000000000001506

RÉSUMÉ :
Objectives To develop and externally validate supervised machine learning models using routinely collected ICU electronic health record (EHR) data to predict short-term onset of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) before clinical diagnosis. Design Prospective observational cohort study with temporal model development and external validation. Setting ICUs. Patients Mechanically ventilated adults in a prospective observational cohort (Successful Clinical Response In Pneumonia Therapy [SCRIPT]) who underwent bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) for suspected pneumonia and had BAL-confirmed adjudicated VAP or adjudicated non-VAP status (n = 507). External validation in the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) included mechanically ventilated patients with greater than or equal to 48 hours of continuous invasive ventilation (n = 2681). Additional evaluation was performed in participants from the Inhaled Amikacin vs. Placebo to Prevent Ventilator Associated Pneumonia (AMIKINHAL) randomized trial evaluating inhaled amikacin for VAP prevention, using centrally adjudicated VAP outcomes. Interventions None. Measurements and main results We extracted 36 day-level clinical features (vital signs, ventilator settings, laboratory values, inflammatory markers, and support devices) from the EHR and trained models to predict VAP within 3, 5, or 7 days before BAL-confirmed diagnosis. Unsupervised clustering identified physiologic states enriched for VAP-labeled days. In internal testing, the best performance reached an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.866 for predicting VAP within 7 days and 0.843 for predicting VAP within 5 days. Predicted risk increased over the days preceding VAP among patients who developed VAP, while remaining low among non-VAP patients. On external validation in MIMIC-IV, the best model achieved an AUROC of 0.817 for predicting VAP within 5 days. Performance was poor in AMIKINHAL, where only nine of 36 features were available. Across models, influential predictors included ventilator duration and mechanics (e.g., positive end-expiratory pressure), platelet count, and inflammatory markers. Conclusions Machine learning models trained on day-level structured ICU data with BAL-confirmed adjudicated labels predicted VAP up to 7 days before clinical diagnosis with strong discrimination and generalized to an external health system when key features were available, supporting future prospective real-time evaluation.

TEXTE INTÉGRAL :
> KEY POINTS
 Question : Can routinely collected ICU electronic health record data predict ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) before clinical diagnosis?
 Findings : In 507 mechanically ventilated adults, machine learning models predicted VAP up to 7 days before diagnosis, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROCs) of 0.866 at 7 days and 0.843 at 5 days. Performance remained strong in Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (AUROC, 0.817 at 5 d) but was attenuated with limited feature availability.
 Meaning : Structured daily ICU data may enable earlier identification of VAP and support prospective evaluation of real-time clinical decision support.
 Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is a common and serious complication among patients requiring mechanical ventilation (). It is associated with increased morbidity, extended ICU stays, and elevated mortality risk (). Despite established guidelines and prevention strategies, early VAP detection remains challenging, as subtle physiologic changes may be masked by the complexity of critical illness. Current clinical tools lack the temporal use and predictive specificity needed to surface early VAP risk in real time (). As a result, clinicians frequently rely on subjective assessments or reactive thresholds, which may not capture emerging infections ().
 Patients requiring mechanical ventilation are closely monitored for infection, with antibiotics typically initiated once VAP is suspected, but often only after clinical deterioration and organ dysfunction. Prophylactic and preventive therapies such as inhaled antibiotics (), selective gastric decontamination (), and systemic antibiotics () to reduce VAP in certain patient populations have been found beneficial in some settings (), but widespread use may increase antibiotic-related complications and antimicrobial resistance. A risk stratification strategy for VAP using electronic health record (EHR) predictive models could identify high-risk patients and assist clinicians in the judicious use of prophylactic therapies to prevent VAP.
 Machine learning (ML) has shown promise in predicting sepsis (), respiratory failure (), and mortality (). However, few studies have focused specifically on early VAP prediction. Furthermore, existing models use static features or coarse temporal resolutions (), limiting clinical utility. We hypothesized that high-quality ICU data, including gold-standard VAP diagnosis based on bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid sampling and clinical adjudication, could support ML models to forecast VAP sufficiently early in the patient’s ICU course to be clinically actionable. Some of this work was previously presented at the 2025 American Thoracic Society Meeting ().
 METHODS
 Cohort and Data Source
 We analyzed data from patients from the Successful Clinical Response In Pneumonia Therapy (SCRIPT) cohort (), a prospective observational study of patients on mechanical ventilation who undergo a BAL for suspected pneumonia. We included data collected from June 2018 to July 2023. Patients’ families or authorized representatives provided informed consent for participation. The study, titled “Successful Clinical Response In Pneumonia Therapy (SCRIPT),” was approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board (STU00204868) on July 27, 2017. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible institutional committee on human experimentation and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975. We used the “CarpeDiem” dataset (), a structured, day-level ICU database that includes vitals, laboratory measurements, support measures, medications, BAL results, and outcome annotations for SCRIPT patients. Data were abstracted from the EHR and study Research Electronic Data Capture forms, as previously described ().
 Pneumonia Adjudication and Labels
 At our hospital, clinicians routinely obtain BAL to evaluate for pneumonia early on in clinical decompensation, with an excellent safety profile (). These were used to label VAP cases, with details of adjudication using a standardized multireviewer adjudication process described in the Supplement ( https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ) and our previous work (). To model VAP onset using the clearest cases, we developed labels based on the temporal relationship between intubated days and BAL-confirmed events ( Fig. A ). As VAP can only develop in intubated patients, only intubated days were used for modeling. Intubated days occurring 3, 5, or 7 days before a confirmed VAP or non-VAP event were labeled as positive or negative, respectively. Additional labeling details are provided in the Supplement ( https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ).

 Features
 Our model used 36 clinical features spanning demographics, vital signs, neurologic status, respiratory mechanics, blood gas values, hematology, blood chemistry, inflammatory markers, medications and interventions, and critical care flags (full list in Supplement, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ). We performed outlier checks, unit harmonization, and quality checks as previously described (). Data missingness is a characteristic of real-world EHR data (); the proportion of missingness across features is summarized in Supplementary Table S1 ( https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ). The models we used are either inherently capable of handling missing data, such as eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), or we performed quartile binning for continuous variables, mask encoding for missingness, and one-hot encoding for categorical variables ( Fig. B ) (, ).
 Sequential Organ Failure Assessment Trends to Predict VAP
 Previous work suggested that trends in Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores could predict the development of subsequent VAP (). We examined this by calculating the change in SOFA score between the first and second intubated days for each patient and examining its relationship with subsequent VAP.
 Unsupervised Phenotyping and Temporal Dynamics
 We explored latent clinical states associated with or without subsequent VAP through unsupervised clustering of daily patient-level feature vectors. Each data point represented a single intubated day and 36 features. Hierarchical clustering with Ward’s linkage was used to generate patient-day clusters, as described in previous work (). Dimensionality reduction with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied to visualize cluster structure and the distribution of VAP and non-VAP days in the embedded space.
 Temporal Feature Dynamics
 To characterize deterioration in the days preceding VAP onset, we identified patients with exactly 5 consecutive intubated days leading up to their first BAL-confirmed pneumonia event. The 36 clinical features were extracted and z score normalized across the cohort. We computed the average value of each feature by day (from day –5 to day –1) and visualized this using a heatmap of feature intensities over time. In addition to magnitude-based trends, we calculated slopes to analyze the steepness of change for each feature between consecutive days.
 Machine Learning, Model Optimization, and Feature Importance
 To assess predictive performance, we implemented an ML framework ( Fig. C ) comprising six model configurations: binned-feature logistic regression (), raw-feature random forest (), XGBoost (), and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) (). For each VAP/non-VAP prediction horizon, data were temporally split into 90% for model development and 10% for held-out testing. Logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost models were trained using 10-fold patient-level cross-validation, while the LSTM was trained on longitudinal sequences of daily feature vectors. Patient-level splits identical to those used for the snapshot-based models were applied to prevent information leakage across sequences. Hyperparameter optimization for all models was performed using Bayesian optimization with Optuna (), with 50 trials per model per task ( Fig. D ). Model performance was evaluated on the held-out test set using standard classification metrics, including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), accuracy, and F1 score. Ninety-five percent CIs for all metrics were calculated using bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations (). Calibration was assessed using calibration curves, calibration slope and intercept, and Brier scores, with additional Brier score decompositions described in the Supplementary Methods ( https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ). Temporal trends in predicted VAP probability and model interpretability were also assessed using model-specific feature-importance methods, including SHapley Additive exPlanations and permutation importance. All interpretability analyses were performed on held-out test data. Additional details on modeling are provided in Supplementary Methods ( https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ). We followed the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis + Artificial Intelligence checklist ().
 External Validation
 We externally validated our models using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) (), which includes ICU admissions from the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center from 2008 to 2022, and the AMIKINHAL randomized controlled trial of inhaled amikacin for VAP prevention (). We labeled VAP cases using similar logic as Agard et al (), while VAP in AMIKINHAL was determined through standardized data collection and central adjudication. d -dimer, procalcitonin, and immunocompromised status were unavailable in MIMIC-IV and were encoded as missing during external validation. Additional details on external datasets and data processing are available in the Supplement ( https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ).
 RESULTS
 Cohort
 Of the 507 mechanically ventilated patients from SCRIPT included in this cohort, 261 (51.5%) experienced a BAL-confirmed episode of VAP, while 246 patients (48.5%) underwent BAL for suspected pneumonia but were adjudicated not to have VAP (“non-VAP”). Detailed inclusion criteria, episode adjudication, and exclusions are described in the Supplement ( https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ). The most frequently detected pathogens were Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa , followed by Enterococcus faecalis , Klebsiella pneumoniae group, and Escherichia coli ( Supplementary Table S2 , https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ). A cohort flow diagram is provided in Supplementary Figure S1 ( https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ), and example pneumonia episode timelines are shown in Supplementary Figure S2 ( https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ).
 Baseline demographic and clinical outcomes of the VAP and non-VAP cohorts are presented in Table . VAP patients had significantly longer ICU stays (28.0 vs. 9.0 d), longer ventilation (27.0 vs. 6.0 d), and higher rates of ECMO use (33.7% vs. 11.8%) and tracheostomy (51.0% vs. 9.3%) than non-VAP patients (all p &lt; 0.001). Discharge home was less common among VAP patients (13.4% vs. 32.9%; p &lt; 0.001).

 Unsupervised Clustering Analysis
 Six-cluster analysis showed that VAP-labeled days were enriched in clusters characterized by elevated inflammatory markers, impaired gas exchange, and increased ventilatory support ( Fig. A ). UMAP projections similarly showed that VAP days localized to distinct regions despite labels being excluded from clustering, indicating coherent physiologic states associated with infection ( Fig. C ). Among 85 patients with 7 consecutive intubated days before their first VAP episode, bilirubin, heart rate, temperature, lymphocyte count, and mean arterial pressure increased from day –5 to day –1, while continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) use, procalcitonin, d -dimer, and lactate dehydrogenase declined ( Fig. B ).

 Early SOFA Change
 SOFA score changes did not differ significantly between VAP and non-VAP groups and showed poor discrimination for subsequent VAP (area under the curve [AUC], 0.528; Supplementary Fig. S3 , https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ).
 Supervised ML Performance
 LSTM models achieved AUCs up to 0.866 for 7-day prediction tasks and average precision scores reaching 0.903, and Random Forest with raw imputation achieved 0.843 AUC for 5-day prediction tasks ( Table ). At a 0.5 threshold, the 5-day raw-feature random forest achieved 82.4% sensitivity, 71.4% specificity, 86.2% positive predictive value, and 65.2% negative predictive value. Calibration analyses showed that resolution exceeded reliability across all prediction horizons, with Brier skill scores of 0.245, 0.298, and 0.271 for the 3-, 5-, and 7-day models, respectively ( Supplementary Fig. S4 , https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ). For the primary 5-day model, Yates decomposition showed minimal mean bias (–0.038; bias squared, 0.001), with residual error driven primarily by correlation deficit (0.095) and variance mismatch (0.055).

 Feature Importance and Model Interpretability
 Feature rankings were consistent across prediction horizons and models, with ventilator mechanics, inflammatory markers, and oxygenation variables emerging as key VAP predictors ( Fig. , A and B ). Ventilator days, plateau pressure, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), and F io 2 were among the most influential features, alongside arterial blood gases, platelet count, and inflammatory markers. After removing ventilator days, Random Forest (raw) performance declined but retained an AUC of 0.7338.

 Temporal Rise in Predicted VAP Risk
 To explore predicted-risk trajectories, we examined probabilities from day –5 to day –1 in a subset of test-set patients. This exploratory analysis included 26 patients: 16 with VAP and 10 without VAP (VAP prevalence, 61.5%). Predicted probabilities in the VAP group remained above the classification threshold of 0.683 and increased modestly from approximately 0.74 on day –5 to 0.79 on day –1 ( Fig. C ). Predicted probabilities in the non-VAP group remained below the threshold. The modest increase may partly reflect cumulative time on mechanical ventilation.
 External Validation in MIMIC-IV and Exploratory Evaluation in AMIKINHAL
 The MIMIC-IV cohort ( Supplementary Figure S5 , https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ) was older, predominantly male, and had lower VAP prevalence and higher mortality than SCRIPT, reflecting differences in ICU exposure and competing risks ( Supplementary Tables S3 and S4 , https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ). Shared feature distributions were directly compared between SCRIPT and MIMIC-IV in Supplementary Figure S6 ( https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ), demonstrating several differences between the cohorts. The MIMIC-IV cohort contained a higher proportion of non-VAP cases, resulting in low baseline AUPRCs of 0.075, 0.104, and 0.123 for the 3-, 5-, and 7-day prediction windows, respectively ( Table ). Despite these differences, raw-feature models maintained AUCs greater than 0.80 across all horizons. In contrast, binned-feature models showed lower AUPRC and F1, particularly at longer horizons, likely reflecting distributional shift because thresholds derived from SCRIPT compressed MIMIC-IV values into fewer bins (Supplementary Fig. S6, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B699 ).

 DISCUSSION
 Despite advances in ICU care and infection control, VAP remains a common complication of mechanical ventilation, and tools to predict its onset are lacking. We developed ML models using day-level ICU data to predict VAP up to 7 days in advance, demonstrating that EHR features can capture evolving clinical signals preceding pneumonia and support proactive risk assessment. We show that unsupervised clustering of patient-days revealed latent phenotypic patterns associated with VAP onset that were enriched for elevated inflammatory markers, increased ventilator pressures, and impaired gas exchange, consistent with the clinical profile of early pneumonia. Our temporal profiling analysis further supports this, showing that respiratory and inflammatory features begin diverging from baseline up to 5 days before diagnosis, with progressively increasing trajectories. The decline in procalcitonin should be interpreted cautiously because of substantial missingness and the inclusion of superinfection VAP occurring during treatment of an earlier pneumonia episode. These changes were not captured by coarse measures like early SOFA score changes alone, which lacked predictive utility, highlighting the need for more granular models in this domain.
 Previous efforts to predict VAP using EHR data have been limited by small sample sizes (), delayed and coarse outcome labels such as International Classification of Diseases codes (), and models that rely on static snapshots (i.e., using data from the beginning of admission only) () rather than dynamic patient trajectories (our day-by-day prediction window). There is also limited external validation in the current studies, with many developed exclusively using the MIMIC-IV dataset (, –). Our study addresses these limitations by leveraging a large cohort of intubated patients with state-of-the-art BAL-confirmed and clinically adjudicated infection status. We used high-resolution temporal data and performed additional unsupervised techniques and feature evaluation to understand physiologic changes over time and examined our model performance in external datasets. In the nature of open science and rigorous reproducibility, we furthermore fully share our code for evaluation and understanding of detailed decisions made during data processing.
 Compared with binned logistic regression, the best-performing ML models had higher AUROCs at 3 days (0.806 vs. 0.725), 5 days (0.843 vs. 0.716), and 7 days (0.866 vs. 0.792). This suggests that nonlinear interactions and temporal patterns may add predictive value beyond traditional regression. However, because the models used different preprocessing approaches, the performance differences cannot be attributed solely to the choice of algorithm. In contrast to many prior studies using static features, diagnosis codes, or a single MIMIC-IV cohort, our study used day-level data, BAL-adjudicated labels, and external validation.
 After tuning with Optuna and evaluating multiple modeling strategies across different labeling windows, predictive performance obtained high AUROCs greater than 0.85. PEEP and F io 2 are important contributors to model prediction. We recognize that these parameters are not specific to VAP and may increase in the setting of other causes of respiratory deterioration, including acute respiratory distress syndrome, atelectasis, and fluid overload. Importantly, these conditions may also coexist with VAP, making complete separation based on ventilator parameters alone challenging. In the SCRIPT study, however, VAP was defined using BAL-based microbiologic confirmation, providing a more specific infection endpoint than ventilator-associated event criteria based solely on sustained increases in PEEP or F io 2 . Thus, the contribution of these variables should be interpreted as markers of evolving respiratory deterioration that may contribute to the recognition of VAP, rather than as VAP-specific features. Further validation in broader populations with more heterogeneous causes of respiratory deterioration will be important to determine the specificity and generalizability of these predictors. Interestingly, several features identified as influential to the model, such as platelet count, often still appeared within the normal laboratory-defined ranges and therefore may be overlooked in busy clinical workflows. These findings highlight the value of ML in surfacing early, multivariate signals that may not reach conventional thresholds for clinical concern. Duration of ventilation likely captures cumulative time at risk for VAP, whereas variables such as PEEP may reflect more immediate respiratory deterioration. These associations are predictive and should not be interpreted as causal.
 Importantly, our models demonstrated robust performance in an external validation cohort derived from MIMIC-IV, despite substantial differences in outcome prevalence, patient population, and feature distributions. While the prevalence of VAP in MIMIC-IV was lower than in SCRIPT, resulting in lower baseline AUPRC values, raw-feature models maintained strong discriminative ability across prediction horizons. Feature importance patterns in the external MIMIC-IV cohort mirrored those observed internally. Duration of mechanical ventilation, neurologic status, and ventilatory mechanics again emerged as the dominant predictors of VAP risk. Although model discrimination and feature importance were similar in MIMIC-IV, differences in outcome definitions mean that these findings do not establish equivalence of the VAP labels. The AMIKINHAL analysis was exploratory because only nine of 36 features were available and the data definitions differed substantially; therefore, it cannot support or refute model generalizability. The relatively poor performance of our model in the AMIKINHAL cohort, which lacked many of these important features, supports this interpretation. Although AUPRC values were lower in MIMIC-IV due to lower VAP prevalence (6.8%, 9.4%, and 11.3% across horizons), model performance remained approximately five-fold (5×) above baseline. This indicates substantial enrichment of true VAP cases despite class imbalance.
 This study has several important limitations. Models were built using data from a single center, using a frequent BAL-sampling protocol, contrary to institutions with different sampling practices, patient populations, or VAP prevention protocols. Because SCRIPT enrolls mechanically ventilated patients undergoing BAL for suspected pneumonia, the cohort is enriched for VAP and does not necessarily represent the overall institutional incidence of VAP, or VAP rates across other populations (). BAL-confirmed VAP was used as the diagnostic standard along with clinician adjudication; however, clinical variability in when BAL is pursued introduces selection bias. This bias is in part mitigated by the regularity with which BAL sampling is performed in our hospital, and the negative control cases where a BAL did not end up showing evidence of pneumonia. Our modeling approach excluded patients with unclear adjudication. Recorded GCS values may reflect neurologic impairment, sedation, or both, which could not be readily distinguished in this dataset; incorporating the doses of sedating medication could be helpful for future work. We used heuristic definitions of VAP/non-VAP in the MIMIC cohort; manual labels by physician experts may have identified different cases of VAP. It is challenging to find external validation cohorts that have both granular EHR data and clean VAP labels. Missingness was substantial and may reflect clinician testing practices, which could differ across institutions. Several important features, including PEEP, F io 2 , GCS, and procalcitonin, are nonspecific or may be affected by sedation, treatment, and other causes of clinical deterioration. Evaluation in AMIKINHAL was limited by the availability of only nine of the 36 model features. Finally, all model evaluations were retrospective, and prospective clinical performance remains unknown. Another limitation is inherent to our patient-level labeling schema, in that patients who experienced both VAP and non-VAP episodes within a short period were not represented in our modeling framework. Although these cases were rare fewer than 2.5% of cases, future work should better focus on even more short-term modeling approaches to capture clinically relevant transitions. Future work should explore prospective deployment for real-time model evaluation integrated with EHR systems, where the model could potentially forecast the short-term development of VAP running on all intubated patient-days in patients not already being treated for VAP.
 CONCLUSIONS
 We found that ML models using structured, day-level ICU data demonstrated strong discrimination for short-term VAP risk. Because calibration varied across prediction horizons, these probabilities should not yet be interpreted as reliable individual-level risk estimates. By leveraging granular physiologic, laboratory, and ventilator parameters, our models identified subtle but consistent changes in patient status up to 7 days before clinical diagnosis. In such patients identified by the model to be high risk for VAP, prophylactic therapies or earlier diagnostics may be beneficial. Prospective validation in diverse, real-time clinical settings will be essential to confirm generalizability and assess implementation impact.
