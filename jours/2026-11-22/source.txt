TITRE : Behavior Change Techniques Associated With Abstinence in a Chat-Based Smoking Cessation Intervention: Secondary Analysis of a Randomized Clinical Trial Using Machine Learning.
REVUE : Journal of medical Internet research · 2026-11-17
DOI : 10.2196/91943

RÉSUMÉ :
Background Isolating the candidate intervention components that drive the success of digital behavioral interventions is crucial for understanding intervention mechanisms and optimizing future interventions. Objective This study aimed to identify behavior change techniques (BCTs) delivered in a chat-based smoking cessation intervention and determine their independent associations with biochemically validated abstinence. Methods This study is a secondary analysis of a pragmatic, cluster-randomized clinical trial conducted in Hong Kong. From June 18 to September 30, 2017, 624 adult daily smokers were recruited from 34 community sites and randomized to the intervention group. Participants received 3 months of personalized, real-time chat-based cessation support delivered by a live counselor via a mobile instant messaging app. The analytic sample included 230 participants (n=170, 73.9% male; mean age 43.0, SD 13.6 years) who engaged with the chat support. All chat dialogues during the real-time support were retrieved and systematically coded using an established BCT taxonomy for smoking cessation (BCTTsm). Four machine learning methods, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression, Extreme Gradient Boosting, the Boruta algorithm, and recursive feature elimination, were used to identify the BCTs that are most predictive of biochemically validated tobacco abstinence outcomes at 3 and 6 months. A prespecified supermajority rule retained only BCTs selected by at least 3 of the 4 models for subsequent analysis. The stability of the selected BCTs was assessed using 500 bootstrap resamples for each model. Poisson regression with robust variance was subsequently used to examine the independent associations between the selected BCTs and abstinence outcomes. Covariates included sex, age, educational level, nicotine dependence, intention to quit in 30 days, and quit attempts at baseline. Results Systematic coding identified 35 distinct BCTs from the BCTTsm as having been delivered. Three BCTs were consistently selected by machine learning as the most predictive of both 3- and 6-month abstinence, with moderate to high stability (ranging from 61% to 100%): "provide rewards contingent on successfully stopping smoking" (BM4), "provide information on withdrawal symptoms" (RC6), and "provide information on consequences of smoking and smoking cessation" (BM1). In multivariable analyses, 6-month abstinence was significantly associated with BM4 (risk ratio [RR] 7.9, 95% CI 4.2-14.6), RC6 (RR 3.3, 95% CI 1.6-6.5), and BM1 (RR 2.7, 95% CI 1.4-5.5), with consistent results for 3-month abstinence. Conclusions Three BCTs, contingent rewards (BM4), information on withdrawal symptoms (RC6), and information on consequences (BM1), were identified as candidate intervention components associated with success in a chat-based smoking cessation intervention. This study is the first to apply machine learning for BCT identification, moving beyond simple frequency counts to data-driven feature selection. These findings provide empirical evidence for the candidate components of effective digital behavioral intervention that may warrant further testing and offer guidance for future intervention optimization.

TEXTE INTÉGRAL :
> Introduction
 Behavior change interventions typically contain multiple components []. The United Kingdom Medical Research Council’s guideline on complex interventions highlights the importance of specifying the “active ingredients” for determining how the intervention achieves its effects []. Precise reporting with sufficient detail of the intervention content is encouraged by the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) and its extension for digital interventions (CONSORT-eHEALTH; Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials of Electronic and Mobile Health Applications and Online Telehealth) statements [,], and the Template for Intervention Description and Replication []. However, digital behavioral interventions are often inadequately or vaguely reported due to the lack of standardized definitions and consistent labels [], which poses challenges in understanding the active components that contribute to intervention effects and hinders the replication of interventions and evidence synthesis [-].
 Behavior change techniques (BCTs), defined as the smallest active and replicable components of an intervention designed to change behavior, are used to specify the intervention content of complex behavioral interventions []. Meta-analyses have shown that specific BCTs identified in interventions for physical activity [], diabetes [], alcohol use [], obesity [], and smoking cessation [], were associated with intervention success. To enhance clarity and standardize the reporting of intervention contents across diverse behavioral domains, the Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy version 1 (BCTTv1) was developed, comprising 93 BCTs grouped into 16 functions []. The BCTTv1 has been widely used for mapping BCTs across various behaviors [-]. Subsequently, a more specific taxonomy for smoking cessation (BCTTsm) was developed, incorporating BCTs such as carbon monoxide (CO) monitoring, advice for coping with urges to smoke, and relapse prevention strategies []. Nevertheless, the active content remains underreported in trials of smoking cessation behavioral interventions [].
 Recent studies have applied these taxonomies to evaluate the BCTs in smoking cessation interventions across different contexts. A meta-analysis that extracted BCTs used in group-based interventions using the BCTTv1 identified 4 BCT domains significantly associated with increased abstinence rates at 6 months, including “problem solving,” “information of health consequences,” “information about social and environmental consequences,” and “reward (outcome)” []. In primary care smoking cessation consultations, only the use of reflective listening (RC7 in BCTTsm) was found to be significantly associated with self-reported 8-week abstinence [].
 Digital-based interventions have demonstrated effectiveness for smoking cessation, including text messaging [] and instant messaging interventions []. Mobile text messaging interventions, such as SmokefreeTXT and Txt2Stop, have been shown to be effective for smoking cessation [], yet the specific messages underlying the intervention effect have remained unclear. Retrospective analyses of the messages found that SmokefreeTXT used 41 out of the 93 BCTs in the BCTTv1 [], while 34 out of 43 BCTs in BCTTsm were found in Txt2Stop []. However, these analyses did not examine the associations of individual BCTs with smoking abstinence, and no studies have systematically identified BCTs in instant messaging interventions for smoking cessation.
 Current evaluations of BCTs in behavioral interventions typically compare outcomes based on the experimental manipulation or observed presence versus absence of specific techniques []. However, behavioral interventions typically deliver multiple interacting BCTs that work synergistically [,], making individual contributions hard to isolate. Machine learning may offer a novel solution for identifying predictive features with reliability and robustness in high-dimensional data []. It has been applied to identify individual-level predictors of smoking cessation, such as participant characteristics [] and app feature use patterns []. However, its potential for systematically prioritizing intervention-level factors, specifically the BCTs most strongly associated with smoking cessation, remains largely unexplored.
 We developed a novel chat-based intervention that provided personalized, real-time smoking cessation support through interaction with a live counselor via mobile instant messaging apps (ie, WhatsApp) []. Our subsequent community-based cluster-randomized controlled trial showed the effectiveness of the chat-based intervention vs brief intervention alone in increasing biochemically validated abstinence rates at 3 months and 6 months []. While greater intervention engagement was generally associated with better outcomes [], engagement and thus the BCTs involved varied widely among participants. This study aimed to (1) systematically code and describe the number and types of BCTs used during the chat-based support, (2) apply machine learning feature selection to identify the BCTs most predictive of biochemically validated abstinence, and (3) quantify the independent associations of these key BCTs with validated abstinence using adjusted regression analyses.
 Methods
 Study Design
 This study was a secondary analysis of a 2-arm, parallel, pragmatic, cluster-randomized clinical trial nested in the “Quit to Win” Smoke-free Community Contest organized by the Hong Kong Council on Smoking and Health. Details of the trial design [] and main results [] have been reported elsewhere. The present study included a prespecified content analysis to identify the BCTs delivered in the chat-based intervention, followed by machine learning to identify BCTs that are most predictive of smoking abstinence. The study was prospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov ( NCT03182790 ) and followed the CONSORT reporting guidelines []. No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this secondary analysis. Harms or unintended effects were not specifically assessed.
 Participants and Analytic Sample
 Participants were proactively recruited from 68 community sites across all 18 districts in Hong Kong from June 18 to September 30, 2017, including the vicinities of shopping malls, residential estates, and transport hubs. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older who had smoked cigarettes daily in the past 3 months (verified by an exhaled CO test of ≥4 parts per million), intended to quit or reduce smoking, and owned a smartphone with an instant messaging app (WhatsApp) installed. Those with physical or cognitive communication barriers or enrolled in other smoking cessation programs were excluded. The sex, age, and daily cigarette consumption were comparable between the trial participants and the general smoking population [].
 In the parent trial, participants were cluster-randomized (1:1) at the community-site level using random permuted block sizes of 2, 4, and 6, with 624 participants assigned to the intervention arm (Figure S1 in ). For this secondary analysis, we focused on the subset of participants who actively engaged with the chat-based support, defined as having sent at least 1 message to the counselor (n=230, 36.9% of the intervention arm). The analytic sample achieved a much higher 6-month biochemically validated abstinence rate than did nonengaged intervention participants (17.4% vs 3.3%). We adopted this approach because the investigation of BCTs as candidate components necessitated their actual delivery and reception within a participant-counselor interaction. Analyzing only engaged participants allowed us to isolate the association between specific therapeutic content delivered and subsequent abstinence outcomes, providing a more direct test of the mechanisms of action within the intervention process.
 Chat-Based Intervention for Smoking Cessation
 All participants in the intervention group received the chat-based intervention for 3 months. Details of the intervention have been reported elsewhere [,]. Briefly, the intervention allowed a live counselor to interact with the participant in real time to provide cessation support via WhatsApp. At least 1 generic instant message related to smoking cessation was sent to the participants each week, and the participants could initiate chat sessions with a live counselor by responding to the messages. The real-time chat support was designed to be dynamic and adaptive based on participants’ specific needs and responses. A counselor used BCTs during the chat sessions to enhance interaction, boost motivation to quit, and encourage the use of adjuvant activities to support smoking cessation. Chat-based support from the live counselors was available during office hours on working days, with prior notice provided to the participants at the beginning.
 Coding Procedures
 We retrieved all chat dialogues between the counselor and intervention group participants from the instant messaging app. Each chat dialogue included the entire chat history of each participant across the entire 3-month intervention period and served as the coding and analytic unit. Two coders (YL and XS), who completed an online BCT taxonomy training [], independently coded the BCTs delivered in the chat dialogues using the BCTTsm []. The coding concordance was discussed prior to independent coding of all chat dialogues. The BCT was coded as either present or absent in each dialogue instead of being counted, since the frequency could be greatly influenced by the intensity of interaction. The method was also used in prior coder training []. Any discrepancies were resolved between the 2 coders via discussion, with an additional investigator (TTL) as the arbitrator. The final agreed-upon BCTs were used for further analysis.
 Measures
 Similar to the parent trial [], the primary outcome was biochemically validated tobacco abstinence at 6 months after randomization, while the key secondary outcome was 3-month (end of treatment) validated abstinence. Abstinence was defined by an exhaled CO level of less than 4 parts per million [], measured by the Bedfont piCO Smokerlyzer (Bedfont Scientific Ltd), and a salivary cotinine level of less than 10 ng/mL, measured by NicAlert (Nymox Pharmaceutical Corp) []. All participants who self-reported at least 7 days of tobacco abstinence at 3 and 6 months and completed biochemical validation received HK $500 (HK $1=US $0.12 as of September 17, 2026) as a "Quit to Win" Contest award.
 At baseline, we used face-to-face questionnaires to collect data on demographics and smoking-related characteristics. These included nicotine dependence, assessed by the Heaviness of Smoking Index; previous quit attempts; intention to quit in 30 days; and perceptions toward quitting (importance, difficulty, and confidence), assessed on a scale from 0 (not at all) to 10 (very much).
 Statistical Analysis
 R (version 4.3.1; R Project for Statistical Computing), RStudio (version 2023.09.1; Posit PBC), and Stata (version 16; StataCorp LLC) were used for statistical analysis. Little test showed that data were missing completely at random ( χ 2 1237 =1204.0; P =.74). Descriptive statistics were used to present the participant characteristics and the frequency of each BCT appearing in the dialogues. Agreement between the 2 coders was assessed by Cohen κ coefficients. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess the collinearity among the BCTs prior to fitting machine learning models. Higher VIF values indicate a higher likelihood of multicollinearity. Any BCTs that were perfectly linearly correlated (ie, aliased) or had a VIF greater than 10 (ie, severe multicollinearity) were removed from the modeling process. All noncollinear BCTs were modeled using supervised machine learning methods to identify those most predictive of 3- and 6-month validated abstinence.
 We used 4 complementary feature selection methods, including wrapper methods (Boruta algorithm and recursive feature elimination [RFE]) and embedded methods (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator [LASSO] regression and Extreme Gradient Boosting [XGBoost]). Wrapper methods evaluate the impact of feature subsets on model performance [], while embedded methods integrate feature selection into the model construction process [,]. These complementary approaches offer strengths in handling high-dimensional data, managing model complexity, and addressing potential overfitting issues. To balance robustness against overfitting with the retention of complementary signals from diverse algorithmic families, we prespecified a supermajority rule prior to model training whereby only BCTs selected by at least 3 of the 4 models were retained for subsequent analysis. Details of the machine learning methods are described in .
 Poisson regression with robust variance was used to estimate the risk ratio (RR) of smoking abstinence by the selected predictive BCTs. Covariates included sex, age, educational level, nicotine dependence, intention to quit in 30 days, and quit attempts at baseline, informed by a systematic review on the predictors of cessation outcomes []. Participants who did not complete follow-ups were assumed to be nonabstinent. To determine whether the associations between the selected predictors and abstinence were due to their unique effects or were affected by the variety of BCTs delivered, we additionally adjusted for the remaining number of BCTs in each dialogue as a sensitivity analysis. A 2-sided P &lt;.05 was considered statistically significant.
 Ethical Considerations
 Ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong and the Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (UW 17‐246). Written informed consent from all participants was obtained prior to data collection. All participants’ data were deidentified to protect privacy and confidentiality. No identifiable information or images of individual participants were included in this manuscript or supplementary materials. Participants were not compensated.

 Discussion
 Principal Findings
 We coded the BCTs used in a real-time chat-based smoking cessation intervention and adopted 4 machine learning feature selection approaches, LASSO, XGBoost, Boruta, and RFE, to identify the BCTs most predictive of abstinence outcomes. A total of 35 unique BCTs were adopted during the intervention. Using multiple machine learning approaches, we consistently identified 3 BCTs that were most predictive of biochemically validated abstinence: “provide rewards contingent on successfully stopping smoking” (BM4), “provide information on withdrawal symptoms” (RC6), and “provide information on consequences of smoking and smoking cessation” (BM1), with moderate to high selection stability across models. The 3 identified BCTs were significantly associated with abstinence outcomes ( P &lt;.001 to P =.004), and the associations remained significant after adjusting for covariates ( P &lt;.001 to P =.01). These findings elucidate the potential components contributing to the effectiveness of chat-based smoking cessation interventions among those who actively engaged with the counseling process.
 To our knowledge, our study is the first in the field to apply machine learning to identify candidate BCTs associated with validated smoking abstinence outcomes in instant messaging interventions. Direct comparison with prior studies is limited by differing contexts, taxonomies, and outcomes. Many studies have only cataloged BCTs in digital interventions without linking them to abstinence [,,,]. One study investigating the BCTs in the treatment manuals of English Stop Smoking Services using the BCTTsm found that BM4 and RC6 were associated with self-reported abstinence []. Using 6-month biochemically validated abstinence, which is regarded as the gold standard outcome in smoking cessation studies, we corroborated the significant associations of BM4 and RC6 with abstinence and additionally found a significant association with BM1.
 The Stop Smoking Services study also identified additional BCTs associated with 4-week abstinence that did not show a significant association with long-term abstinence in our study. This discrepancy may reflect the role of these BCTs in facilitating early behavior change. For example, “measure CO” (BM11) can boost an individual’s immediate motivation to quit but may not translate into sustained long-term behavior change []. Techniques such as “summarize information or confirm client decisions” (RC9) and “use reflective listening” (RC7) may be more effective in face-to-face interventions, where direct interaction and emotional support are possible but are limited in mobile health (mHealth) approaches.
 “Providing rewards contingent on successfully stopping smoking” (BM4) incorporated both praise and financial incentives in our intervention. It leveraged extrinsic motivation to encourage behavior change. By validating progress and efforts in quitting, praise and encouragement can boost these individuals’ self-efficacy, a known facilitator of abstinence []. Financial incentives offer a direct extrinsic motivator [] and may improve intervention engagement [], leading to higher abstinence rates []. Beyond financial rewards, gamified interventions, such as token or point-based systems, could be considered as more cost-effective and scalable alternatives for integration into mHealth platforms to facilitate smoking cessation []. Though the consistent prioritization of BM4 across machine learning approaches provides convergent evidence for its potential effect, BM4 is inherently reactive, which may reflect an already favorable trajectory among participants. Future investigations, such as microrandomization trials, could help disentangle its therapeutic role, while digital payment platforms (eg, Alipay and WeChat Pay) offer a scalable means of delivering contingent rewards in mHealth interventions.
 Both “provide information on withdrawal symptoms” (RC6) and “provide information on consequences” (BM1) likely support cessation by strengthening an individual’s capability to quit, a core component of the Behavior Change Wheel []. RC6 equips individuals with knowledge and coping strategies for managing withdrawal symptoms that threaten self-efficacy and increase relapse risk [,]. BM1 functions by shaping key theoretical determinants of behavior, including perceived susceptibility to smoking and the benefits of quitting (Health Belief Model), and by altering behavioral beliefs (Theory of Planned Behavior) []. The differential selection consistency of RC6 and BM1 across models, with their attenuation in sensitivity analyses, suggests that the associations of these BCTs may be partially confounded by participants’ preexisting motivation and self-belief. Moreover, the knowledge-behavior gap indicates that health information alone is rarely sufficient to facilitate behavior change []. Combining informational content with actionable strategies (eg, problem solving and action planning) may therefore produce synergistic effects. Future research could explore BCT combinations to optimize intervention effects.
 The application of machine learning feature selection offers several methodological insights. The use of multiple complementary algorithms with bootstrap resampling enhanced the accuracy and reliability of our results, while the supermajority rule improved reproducibility. We demonstrated the value of machine learning for generating data-driven hypotheses about candidate intervention components in complex, real-world instant messaging interventions, though experimental designs are needed to confirm these findings.
 Limitations
 The study has several limitations. First, our analysis focused on participants who engaged with the real-time chat support. This restriction was methodologically necessary since nonengaged participants received no BCTs; including them would conflate the effect of engagement with those of specific BCTs. However, this introduces selection bias, as engaged and nonengaged participants differed significantly in characteristics that predict abstinence. Although we adjusted for these characteristics, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Therefore, our findings are conditional on engagement and speak primarily to the mechanisms of action within an active therapeutic process. Second, our coding only recorded the presence (not frequency) of BCTs, as evidence suggests that frequency may not indicate effective engagement and treatment success in digital health []. In chat-based interventions, frequency may be influenced by nonprognostic factors, such as participant verbosity and interaction style. Nevertheless, future studies could examine whether a dose-response relationship exists for specific BCTs. Third, our study focused only on counselor-delivered BCTs and did not code participants’ responses. Future studies examining both sides of the conversation would better capture the dynamic, interactive nature of chat-based counseling. Fourth, several BCTs in the BCTTsm did not appear in the chat dialogues and could not be evaluated. These include BCTs that require in-person contact (eg, measure CO [BM11] and offer or direct toward appropriate written materials [RC5]) and are thus less relevant to chat-based support. Others (eg, BM8 and BM10) did not arise naturally during the chat-based support, as our intervention was designed to be adaptive and responsive rather than prescriptive. Fifth, although BCTs were coded from the intervention period preceding the outcome assessments, we cannot rule out that some BCTs (eg, BM4) were delivered in response to participant progress and may partly reflect a positive trajectory. However, even reactive reinforcement may still have therapeutic value by supporting continued abstinence. Finally, our analysis did not adjust for multiplicity given its exploratory nature. The findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and confirmatory studies are needed.
 Conclusions
 While mobile messaging has been proven effective for smoking cessation, the intervention content contributing to treatment success remains poorly understood. By characterizing the BCTs in an effective chat-based smoking cessation intervention, we found that providing rewards contingent on successfully stopping smoking (BM4), information on withdrawal symptoms (RC6), and information on consequences of smoking and smoking cessation (BM1) were significantly associated with biochemically validated tobacco abstinence. Unlike prior BCT studies that relied on frequency counts, this study demonstrates the value of machine learning for identifying BCTs associated with treatment success. These findings offer a more precise understanding of candidate intervention components rather than treating the intervention as a black box. While further research is warranted to validate the findings, these 3 BCTs represent promising targets for optimizing similar future treatment models, including chatbots.
