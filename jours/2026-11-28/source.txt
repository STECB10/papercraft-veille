TITRE : Comparison of Retinopathy Screening Performed Using Conventional Methods and an Artificial Intelligence-Assisted System in Prediabetic and Diabetic Patients: A Single-Center Prospective Analysis.
REVUE : Medicina (Kaunas, Lithuania) · 2026-11-18
DOI : 10.3390/medicina62112267

RÉSUMÉ :
Background and Objectives : AI-assisted retinal imaging is increasingly proposed to expand diabetic retinopathy (DR) screening capacity, but most validation studies use reading-center standards, exclude prediabetes, and assess macular edema against photographic surrogates rather than optical coherence tomography (OCT). We compared an AI-assisted retinal screening system with routine ophthalmological examination in an unselected internal medicine screening population. Materials and Methods : In this prospective, cross-sectional diagnostic accuracy study, consecutive adults with prediabetes or diabetes underwent AI-assisted retinal imaging (index test) followed by masked ophthalmological examination (reference standard), with OCT as the reference for macular edema. Analyses were performed at the eye level ( n = 714) and patient level ( n = 357) using Wilson score 95% confidence intervals and Cohen kappa (κ) for agreement. Results : Among 357 patients (714 eyes; 83.8% diabetes, 16.2% prediabetes), DR prevalence by ophthalmological examination was 15.0% (eyes) and 17.9% (patients), versus 24.4% and 33.1% by AI (McNemar p p = 0.040); false negatives had milder disease. In prediabetes, DR prevalence was 1.7% and agreement was absent (κ = -0.03). AI sensitivity for OCT-defined macular edema was 45.5% (κ = 0.197), with no significant central macular thickness difference by AI status. Conclusions : The AI system showed high negative predictive value but systematically over-called DR, with only fair-to-moderate agreement and performance below accepted screening benchmarks, particularly in prediabetes and for macular edema. These findings support its use as a rule-out triage adjunct with ophthalmological confirmation of positive results, rather than an autonomous substitute, and argue for local recalibration before deployment.

TEXTE INTÉGRAL :
> 1. Introduction
 Diabetes mellitus has become one of the defining chronic disease burdens of this century. According to the eleventh edition of the International Diabetes Federation Diabetes Atlas, an estimated 589 million adults aged 20–79 years were living with diabetes in 2024, corresponding to 11.1% of the world’s adult population, with a projected increase to approximately 853 million by 2050 [,]. Prediabetes, the intermediate state of dysglycemia that precedes overt diabetes, affects a comparable or larger number of individuals and is increasingly recognized as a period in which microvascular injury may already be underway rather than a purely benign risk state [].
 Diabetic retinopathy (DR) remains the leading cause of preventable vision loss in working-age adults and is the most specific microvascular complication of diabetes [,,]. Because early and moderate DR is typically asymptomatic, timely detection depends entirely on systematic screening, and both international and national guidelines therefore recommend regular retinal examination for all patients with diabetes. The American Diabetes Association Standards of Care recommend an initial dilated and comprehensive eye examination at, or shortly after, diagnosis, with subsequent examinations at intervals determined by the presence of retinopathy and by glycemic control []. In practice, however, adherence to these recommendations is limited worldwide: pooled estimates indicate that only about two-thirds of patients in high-income countries and fewer than half in low- and middle-income countries receive guideline-recommended eye examinations, constrained by the number of ophthalmologists available, by geographical access, and by the competing demands placed on eye services [,].
 Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted analysis of retinal images has emerged as the most mature clinical application of machine learning in medicine and has been proposed as a means of closing this screening gap []. The pivotal trial of the first autonomous system to receive regulatory clearance in the United States reported a sensitivity of 87.2% and a specificity of 90.7% for more-than-mild DR against a rigorous reading-center standard [], and a subsequent pivotal evaluation of a second autonomous system reported comparable performance []. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis restricted to regulator-approved deep learning systems, encompassing 82 studies and more than 880,000 examinations, pooled a sensitivity of 93% and a specificity of 90% per patient against human grading []. On the strength of such data, current guidance explicitly permits interpretation of screening retinal photographs by AI programs that have been approved for this purpose [].
 Performance in controlled validation datasets, however, has repeatedly failed to translate directly into routine practice. In a multicenter head-to-head comparison of seven automated systems evaluated against an arbitrated real-world dataset, sensitivities ranged from 50.98% to 85.90%, and most algorithms performed no better than human graders []. Screening program evaluations have reported sensitivities for referable disease of around 78% [], and opportunistic screening in endocrinology and community settings has shown that a substantial proportion of encounters yield ungradable images []. Commentators have drawn attention to the referral burden generated when positive predictive value is low []. Three specific gaps persist in this literature. First, most validation studies use standardized photographic grading, often by a reading center, as the comparator, whereas the decision that an AI system is intended to influence in everyday practice is the one that would otherwise be made by an ophthalmologist at the slit lamp. Second, individuals with prediabetes are almost universally excluded, despite evidence that retinal microvascular change can begin before the diagnostic threshold for diabetes is crossed [] and despite the fact that such patients are commonly referred for screening in internal medicine practice. Third, diabetic macular edema (DME) is usually assessed against fundus-photographic surrogates such as hard exudates rather than against optical coherence tomography (OCT), which is the accepted structural standard [,].
 We therefore conducted a prospective, single-center diagnostic accuracy study in an unselected internal medicine screening pathway. The primary objective was to determine the diagnostic accuracy of, and the level of agreement achieved by, an AI-assisted retinal screening system for the detection of DR, using masked ophthalmological examination as the reference standard. Secondary objectives were to quantify agreement for three-category severity grading, to evaluate AI performance for macular edema against OCT, to examine performance in clinically relevant subgroups including prediabetes, and to identify clinical determinants of discordance between the AI system and the ophthalmologist.
 2. Materials and Methods
 2.1. Study Design and Setting
 This was a prospective, cross-sectional diagnostic accuracy study conducted in the internal medicine and ophthalmology clinics of Adana City Training and Research Hospital, Adana, Türkiye. The study is reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) 2015 statement []. The protocol was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Adana City Training and Research Hospital (approval date: 19 February 2026; meeting number 22, decision number 1137) and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.
 2.2. Participants
 Consecutive adults attending the internal medicine outpatient clinic between 23 February 2026 and 23 April 2026 with an established diagnosis of prediabetes or diabetes mellitus, and referred to the ophthalmology clinic for DR screening, were assessed for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of prediabetes or diabetes mellitus according to prevailing American Diabetes Association criteria [], age of 18 years or older, and provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were failure to meet the diagnostic criteria for prediabetes or diabetes, a previously established diagnosis of DR, a coexisting retinal or macular disorder that could independently cause macular edema or interfere with the assessment of diabetic retinal disease (retinal vein occlusion, epiretinal membrane, age-related macular degeneration or postoperative cystoid macular edema), and refusal to participate. Participants were recruited consecutively in order to reproduce the case mix encountered in routine practice and to avoid the spectrum bias that arises when validation cohorts are enriched for disease. With the observed patient-level DR prevalence of 17.9%, the achieved sample size allowed a sensitivity of 75% to be estimated with a 95% confidence interval half-width of approximately 10.5 percentage points and a specificity of 76% with a half-width of approximately 4.9 percentage points, which was judged adequate for the descriptive objectives of the study.
 2.3. Index Test: Artificial Intelligence-Assisted Retinal Screening
 All participants first underwent AI-assisted retinal imaging. Non-mydriatic fundus photographs were obtained using the Topcon TRC-NW400 non-mydriatic retinal camera (Top Ophthalmic Products &amp; Services Corp., Makati City, Philippines), with one macula-centered and one optic disc-centered 45° field per eye, and were analyzed automatically by the EyeCheckup artificial intelligence software (EyeCheckup Ltd., London, UK; developed by Ural Telecommunication Inc., Antalya, Türkiye), version 1.0, which is CE-marked as a Class IIa medical device. In a published validation using the same camera model, the software showed a sensitivity of 95.2% and a specificity of 96.5% for more-than-mild DR []. The software reports an image quality score for each photograph and, for each eye, a DR severity level (no apparent DR, mild, moderate or severe non-proliferative DR [NPDR], or proliferative DR [PDR]) together with the presence or absence of suspected clinically significant macular edema. For analysis, the per-eye severity output was collapsed into three pre-specified categories (grade 0, no DR; grade 1, mild or moderate NPDR; grade 2, severe NPDR or PDR), and the macular edema output was used as a binary variable. The software provides categorical outputs and has no user-adjustable decision threshold. Its intended use is the detection of more-than-mild and vision-threatening DR; in this study, however, the index test was pre-specified as positive for any DR (grade ≥ 1, including mild NPDR), which is a lower threshold than the manufacturer’s intended referral threshold, so that its performance could be compared directly with the ophthalmologist’s detection of any DR. Imaging and automated analysis were performed by a technician who had received standardized training in image acquisition, and the AI output was generated before and independently of the ophthalmological examination.
 2.4. Reference Standard: Ophthalmological Examination
 Following AI assessment, all participants were examined independently by two experienced ophthalmologists using conventional methods, namely slit-lamp biomicroscopy and dilated fundus examination. The interval between AI imaging and the ophthalmological examination was 0–5 days, and no clinical intervention was performed in between. The examining ophthalmologists were masked to the AI output and to each other’s findings. DR severity was recorded for each eye on the same three-category scale used for the index test. Disagreements between the two examiners were resolved by consensus. Pupil dilation was achieved with topical tropicamide 1% (Tropamid) as part of the routine ophthalmological examination. The ophthalmological examination was designated the reference standard for the presence and severity of DR.
 2.5. Optical Coherence Tomography
 Macular imaging was performed using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT; RTVue XR Avanti, Optovue Inc., Fremont, CA, USA) with both the Retina Map and 3D Clinical scanning protocols, covering a 6 × 6 mm area centered on the fovea. All OCT recordings were obtained under mydriasis by the same experienced technician using the same device. Central macular thickness (CMT) was measured and recorded quantitatively, while OCT images were assessed for retinal thickening and the presence of intraretinal and/or subretinal fluid. Diabetic macular edema (DME) was diagnosed based on increased CMT and/or the presence of intraretinal or subretinal fluid, after excluding other potential causes of macular edema. OCT images were evaluated by two investigators (K.G.O. and A.E.D.). The presence of macular edema was recorded at the patient level as a binary variable. CMT was measured separately for each eye in micrometers. Because the reference macular variables were defined at the patient level, and because the two eyes of the same individual cannot be treated as statistically independent, the higher of the two eye-specific values (the “worse eye”) was used for each patient in analyses of CMT.
 2.6. Variables
 Demographic variables (age, sex), glycemic variables (prediabetes or diabetes status, diabetes duration, glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c], fasting blood glucose), clinical variables (systolic and diastolic blood pressure, hypertension, dyslipidemia, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [ASCVD], diabetic polyneuropathy, chronic kidney disease, tobacco or alcohol use) and laboratory parameters (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR], urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio [ACR], lipid profile, hemogram and liver enzymes) were extracted from hospital records. A composite comorbidity count was derived from five principal conditions (hypertension, dyslipidemia, ASCVD, diabetic polyneuropathy and chronic kidney disease).
 2.7. Statistical Analysis
 Analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA); confidence intervals based on the Wilson score method, weighted kappa statistics, McNemar tests and bootstrap procedures were computed with Python 3.12 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) using the SciPy and statsmodels libraries. Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median (minimum–maximum) or median (interquartile range [IQR]); categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages.
 Diagnostic accuracy was assessed at two levels: eye level ( n = 714) and patient level ( n = 357). At the patient level, DR was defined as retinopathy detected in at least one eye. Using ophthalmological examination as the reference standard, 2 × 2 cross-tabulations were constructed and sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV) and accuracy were calculated. Two-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived using the Wilson score method, which provides better coverage than the normal approximation when proportions approach the boundaries of the scale or when cell counts are small []. Because the two eyes of an individual are correlated, eye-level estimates were additionally accompanied by CIs obtained from a non-parametric bootstrap that resampled patients rather than eyes (4000 replicates), presented as a sensitivity analysis.
 Agreement between the AI system and the ophthalmologist was quantified using the Cohen kappa coefficient (κ), interpreted according to the criteria of Landis and Koch (0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial) []. Agreement for three-category severity grading was assessed using unweighted, linearly weighted and quadratically weighted kappa, with bootstrap 95% CIs. Systematic differences in the classification rate between the two methods were tested with the McNemar test.
 For macular edema, OCT was the reference standard. Because OCT data were recorded as a single patient-level variable, the eye-level AI macular edema outputs were reduced to the patient level using an “at least one eye positive” rule. Pre-specified subgroup analyses of patient-level accuracy were performed by glycemic status, age (&lt;60 versus ≥60 years), sex, HbA1c (&lt;8% versus ≥8%), diabetes duration (&lt;5 versus ≥5 years) and comorbidity burden (&lt;2 versus ≥2 conditions).
 Determinants of discordance were examined among patients classified as free of DR by the ophthalmologist, comparing those incorrectly classified as positive by the AI system with those correctly classified as negative. Group comparisons used the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, given non-normal distributions and unequal group sizes, and the Fisher exact test for categorical variables. Variables with a plausible mechanistic relationship to retinal microvascular change were then entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression model with false-positive classification as the dependent variable; ACR was log-transformed because of marked right skew. CMT was compared between groups with the Mann–Whitney U test. A two-sided p value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons, and subgroup and discordance analyses should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.
 3. Results
 3.1. Study Population and Clinical Characteristics
 Of 640 patients assessed for eligibility, 283 were excluded (83 did not meet the diagnostic criteria for prediabetes or diabetes, 107 had a previously established diagnosis of DR, and 93 declined participation), leaving 357 patients (714 eyes) in the analysis (). The mean age of participants was 55.7 ± 10.3 years (median 56, range 24–88) and 188 (52.7%) were women. Diabetes mellitus was present in 299 patients (83.8%) and prediabetes in 58 (16.2%). Among patients with diabetes, the mean disease duration was 8.2 ± 6.6 years, and the mean HbA1c for the cohort was 8.0 ± 2.0%. The most frequent accompanying conditions were dyslipidemia (65.8%) and hypertension (63.9%), followed by ASCVD (33.3%) and diabetic polyneuropathy (16.5%). Demographic, clinical and laboratory characteristics are presented in . No adverse events occurred during retinal imaging or the ophthalmological examinations.

 3.2. Diagnostic Performance for the Detection of Diabetic Retinopathy
 On ophthalmological examination, DR was identified in 15.0% of eyes (107 of 714) and in 17.9% of patients (64 of 357). The AI system reported DR considerably more often, in 24.4% of eyes (174 of 714) and 33.1% of patients (118 of 357). This difference in classification rate was systematic rather than random: the McNemar test was significant at both eye level (99 versus 32 discordant pairs, p &lt; 0.001) and patient level (70 versus 16 discordant pairs, p &lt; 0.001), indicating a consistent tendency of the AI system to over-call retinopathy relative to the reference standard.
 Diagnostic performance is summarized in and displayed with the corresponding 2 × 2 distributions in . At the eye level, sensitivity was 70.1% (95% CI 60.8–77.9) and specificity 83.7% (80.5–86.4). NPV was high at 94.1% (91.8–95.8), whereas PPV was low at 43.1% (36.0–50.5); overall accuracy was 81.7% (78.6–84.3). At the patient level, sensitivity was slightly higher at 75.0% (63.2–84.0) but specificity fell to 76.1% (70.9–80.6), with an NPV of 93.3% (89.4–95.8), a PPV of 40.7% (32.2–49.7) and an accuracy of 75.9% (71.2–80.1). Agreement between the two methods was moderate at the eye level (κ = 0.428, 95% CI 0.339–0.516) and fair at the patient level (κ = 0.384, 0.271–0.498) []. Ninety-nine false-positive and 32 false-negative eyes were observed, corresponding to 70 false-positive and 16 false-negative patients. When eye-level CIs were recomputed with a patient-level cluster bootstrap to account for the correlation between fellow eyes, the intervals widened only modestly (sensitivity 70.1%, 58.9–80.0; specificity 83.7%, 80.2–87.0; PPV 43.1%, 33.9–52.5; NPV 94.1%, 91.5–96.4; κ = 0.428, 0.327–0.523), and no conclusion was altered.

 3.3. Agreement in Retinopathy Severity Grading
 Agreement for the three-category severity scale (grade 0, no DR; grade 1, mild or moderate NPDR; grade 2, severe NPDR or PDR) was fair to moderate, with an unweighted κ of 0.407 (95% CI 0.331–0.480); linearly and quadratically weighted coefficients were essentially identical (κ = 0.402 and 0.393, respectively), indicating that disagreements were not concentrated at the extremes of the scale. The eye-level cross-classification is shown in . The AI system assigned a higher severity grade than the ophthalmologist in 102 eyes and a lower grade in 36 eyes; it classified 158 eyes as grade 1 compared with 99 by the ophthalmologist, and 16 eyes as grade 2 compared with 8. The number of eyes graded as severe disease was small (8 eyes by the reference standard), so performance for vision-threatening disease could not be estimated with useful precision.

 3.4. Subgroup Analyses
 Patient-level performance in pre-specified subgroups is presented in . Performance varied most markedly by glycemic status. Among the 299 patients with diabetes, sensitivity was 76.2% (64.4–85.0), specificity 72.9% (66.9–78.2) and κ = 0.382. Among the 58 patients with prediabetes, only one patient had DR on ophthalmological examination (prevalence 1.7% versus 21.1% in diabetes, p &lt; 0.001); this single case was not detected by the AI system, and six of 57 patients without retinopathy were classified as positive; agreement in this subgroup was therefore absent (κ = −0.03).

 Performance also varied by glycemic control. In patients with HbA1c ≥ 8%, sensitivity was 88.2% (73.4–95.3) but specificity fell to 64.8% (55.3–73.2), whereas in patients with HbA1c &lt; 8% sensitivity was only 60.0% (42.3–75.4) with a specificity of 82.1% (75.9–86.9). A similar but weaker gradient was observed across diabetes duration (sensitivity 80.0% at ≥5 years versus 66.7% at &lt;5 years). Performance did not differ appreciably by sex and was marginally better in patients aged 60 years or older (κ = 0.447) than in younger patients (κ = 0.339).
 3.5. Clinical Determinants of Discordance
 Among the 293 patients classified as free of DR by the ophthalmologist, 70 (23.9%) were classified as positive by the AI system. As shown in , these patients had a substantially worse metabolic and microvascular profile than concordantly negative patients: higher HbA1c (8.2 ± 1.8% versus 7.6 ± 1.8%, p = 0.004), higher fasting blood glucose (170.7 ± 80.9 versus 146.7 ± 60.2 mg/dL, p = 0.014), higher diastolic blood pressure (80.5 ± 14.6 versus 76.1 ± 12.1 mmHg, p = 0.016) and a higher urinary ACR (median 13.6 [IQR 7.0–40.8] versus 8.6 [4.2–28.3] mg/g, p = 0.007). Diabetes, rather than prediabetes, was more frequent among false-positive patients (91.4% versus 77.1%, p = 0.009). In a multivariable logistic regression model adjusting for age, sex, glycemic status and HbA1c, the log-transformed ACR remained independently associated with false-positive classification (odds ratio 1.23 per log unit, 95% CI 1.01–1.50, p = 0.040), whereas none of the other covariates reached significance.

 3.6. Assessment of Macular Edema
 Macular edema was detected by OCT in 22 patients (6.2%) and reported by the AI system in 53 patients (14.8%). With OCT as the reference standard, AI sensitivity for macular edema was 45.5% (95% CI 26.9–65.3), specificity 87.2% (83.2–90.3), PPV 18.9% (10.6–31.4), NPV 96.1% (93.2–97.7) and accuracy 84.6% (80.5–88.0). Agreement was slight (κ = 0.197, 95% CI 0.001–0.392), with 43 false-positive and 12 false-negative classifications; as for DR, the excess of positive calls was systematic (McNemar p &lt; 0.001).
 3.7. Central Macular Thickness
 Mean CMT in the 354 patients with evaluable measurements was 256.2 ± 37.5 µm (median 252). As expected, CMT was significantly higher in patients with OCT-defined macular edema than in those without (302.7 ± 80.4 versus 253.2 ± 31.0 µm, p = 0.002). CMT was also significantly higher in patients in whom the ophthalmologist detected DR (275.1 ± 59.6 versus 252.1 ± 29.3 µm, p = 0.013) and increased monotonically across ophthalmologist-assigned severity grades (252.1 ± 29.3, 271.7 ± 56.3 and 307.0 ± 84.7 µm for grades 0, 1 and 2, respectively; p = 0.031). By contrast, CMT did not differ significantly between patients in whom the AI system reported retinopathy and those in whom it did not (264.3 ± 49.2 versus 252.2 ± 29.4 µm, p = 0.229).  4. Discussion
 In this prospective single-center study of 357 consecutive patients with prediabetes or diabetes screened in an internal medicine pathway, an AI-assisted retinal screening system detected DR with moderate sensitivity and specificity and only fair-to-moderate agreement with masked ophthalmological examination. Four findings deserve emphasis. First, the system systematically over-called retinopathy, reporting DR in roughly twice as many patients as the reference standard, which translated into a PPV of approximately 41% at the patient level. Second, the NPV was consistently high, at 93–94%. Third, performance was strongly modified by clinical context, being highest in patients with poor glycemic control and effectively nil in prediabetes. Fourth, agreement for OCT-defined macular edema was slight, and the AI system’s positive retinopathy calls were not significantly associated with macular thickness.
 4.1. Diagnostic Accuracy in the Context of Screening Benchmarks
 Established national screening programs have historically operated to quality standards requiring a sensitivity of at least 80% together with a specificity of at least 90–95% []. The performance observed here (sensitivity 70–75%, specificity 76–84%) falls short of both thresholds. This contrasts with regulatory and pivotal trial data, in which sensitivities and specificities close to 90% have been reported [,], and with the pooled estimates of 93% sensitivity and 90% specificity derived from meta-analysis of regulator-approved systems []. It is, however, consistent with a growing body of real-world evidence. In a multicenter head-to-head validation of seven automated systems against an arbitrated dataset, sensitivities ranged from 50.98% to 85.90% and most systems did not outperform human graders []. A screening program evaluation in the Caribbean reported a sensitivity of 77.5% and a specificity of 91.5% for referable disease []. At the favorable end of the spectrum, a real-world comparison in the Czech Republic reported an AI sensitivity of 92.1% and a specificity of 90.7% against an expert board, exceeding the performance of both general ophthalmologists and retina specialists []; a clinical-practice evaluation in Switzerland reported high accuracy for autonomous screening []; and a large regional program in the Canary Islands reported near-perfect binocular sensitivity with kappa values above 0.92 against ophthalmologist grading []. The distance between these reports and our own findings is best explained not by a single factor but by differences in the target condition (any DR versus referable or vision-threatening DR), in the reference standard, in the imaging protocol, in the camera platform [] and in the case mix of the screened population; formal meta-regression has confirmed that the DR severity threshold, image gradability, pupil dilation, diagnostic criteria and reference standard together account for most of the heterogeneity between studies [].
 4.2. Systematic Over-Detection and Its Operational Consequences
 The most consistent signal in our data was directional. At every level of analysis, the AI system produced more positive calls than the reference standard, and the McNemar test confirmed that this asymmetry was systematic. A comparable pattern of severity over-estimation has been described even in studies reporting otherwise excellent agreement []. From a screening perspective, a bias towards over-detection is preferable to the reverse, because the principal harm of a screening failure is undetected sight-threatening disease. The operational consequences are nonetheless substantial. In a population resembling ours, autonomous use of the system would generate approximately 33 referrals for every 100 patients screened, of which about 20 would prove unnecessary on specialist examination. This closely mirrors the concern raised in relation to a primary care implementation in which a sensitivity of 100% was accompanied by a specificity of 82% []: the resulting positive predictive value of 19% meant that four of every five referred patients did not have referable disease, with a corresponding burden on ophthalmology services, on primary care and on patients themselves []. Recent commentary has argued that this trade-off is best managed by shifting the operating point of screening algorithms from any DR towards vision-threatening DR, which is the outcome that screening programs exist to prevent [].
 4.3. The Reference Standard and the Interpretation of Discordance
 Any comparison of this kind is constrained by the imperfection of the comparator. We deliberately chose masked ophthalmological examination as the reference standard because it represents the decision that AI-assisted screening is intended to influence in this care pathway, but dilated biomicroscopy and photographic grading are known to disagree in both directions. Reported sensitivities of single-field fundus photography against dilated ophthalmoscopy range from 38% to 100% [], and multi-field photography can detect lesions that are overlooked at the slit lamp, whereas ophthalmoscopy retains an advantage for peripheral and three-dimensional findings; in a large subgroup analysis comparing an AI system with ophthalmologists’ dilated examinations against reading-center grading, both the algorithm and the clinicians diverged appreciably from the photographic standard []. Substantial variability between expert graders themselves has also been documented and is a recognized limitation of any single-reader reference standard []. Discordant classifications in our study cannot therefore be attributed with certainty to error on the part of the algorithm.
 Our analysis of discordance provides an empirical argument that at least some apparent false positives were not simply noise. Patients classified as positive by the AI system but negative by the ophthalmologist had significantly higher HbA1c, higher fasting glucose, higher diastolic blood pressure and higher urinary ACR, and the association with ACR persisted after multivariable adjustment. Albuminuria is a marker of generalized microvascular injury that shares pathophysiological mechanisms with retinal capillary disease, and its independent association with AI positivity in eyes judged normal at the slit lamp is more consistent with the detection of early, subtle retinal lesions than with random misclassification. The converse pattern reinforces this interpretation: patients missed by the AI system had lower HbA1c and shorter diabetes duration, and almost all had mild disease, which is the pattern expected when an algorithm’s threshold is calibrated against lesion burden. A definitive resolution would require adjudication of discordant cases against Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) seven-field stereoscopic photography [], which was beyond the scope of this study but should be built into future work.
 4.4. Performance in Prediabetes
 To our knowledge, few prospective evaluations of AI-assisted retinal screening have included patients with prediabetes. In our cohort, DR prevalence in this group was 1.7% by ophthalmological examination, whereas the AI system classified 10.5% of ophthalmologist-negative prediabetic patients as positive, and agreement was absent. This is an arithmetically predictable consequence of applying a test with imperfect specificity in a very-low-prevalence setting, where PPV collapses regardless of how well the algorithm performs. It nevertheless carries a practical message. Although retinal microvascular change can begin before the diagnostic threshold for diabetes is crossed, and although population studies have reported retinopathy in a small but appreciable minority of individuals with impaired glucose tolerance [], current guidelines do not recommend routine retinal screening in this group [], and our data indicate that indiscriminate application of an AI system at its diabetes-calibrated operating point would generate a high false-positive yield with negligible diagnostic return. If AI-assisted screening is to be extended to prediabetes, it will require either a different operating point or restriction to individuals with additional microvascular risk markers.
 4.5. Macular Edema and Central Macular Thickness
 Performance for macular edema was clearly weaker than for retinopathy, with a sensitivity of 45.5% and slight agreement against OCT. This is unsurprising in mechanistic terms. Two-dimensional color fundus photography cannot visualize retinal thickening directly, and conventional grading relies on hard exudates as a surrogate, a proxy known to have poor sensitivity and poor positive predictive value for center-involving edema []. Dedicated deep learning systems trained specifically on OCT-derived labels have achieved sensitivities and specificities of approximately 80% and have outperformed expert graders using photographs alone [], which indicates that the limitation lies in the training target and imaging modality rather than in the technology as such. The CMT findings add a further layer of evidence. CMT rose significantly across ophthalmologist-assigned severity grades and differed significantly according to ophthalmologist-determined DR status, whereas no significant difference was found according to AI-determined status. This absence of a significant difference does not establish equivalence between the groups, and DR may be present without increased CMT.
 4.6. Clinical Implications
 Taken together, our findings support a specific and limited role for AI-assisted retinal screening in this setting. The high NPV means that a negative AI result provides useful reassurance and could reasonably be used to extend screening intervals, an approach already sanctioned by guidelines for patients without retinopathy whose glycemic indicators are within the goal range [], or to prioritize patients within a constrained ophthalmology service; approximately two-thirds of our cohort fell into this category. Conversely, the low PPV means that a positive AI result should be regarded as an indication for ophthalmological examination rather than as a diagnosis and should not by itself trigger treatment pathways. Such a hybrid model, in which the algorithm performs triage and the ophthalmologist confirms positives, preserves the efficiency gains of automation while containing the referral burden, and has been shown to improve the performance of teleophthalmology workflows []; economic analyses indicate that automated pre-screening of this kind can be cost-effective relative to fully manual grading [,]. Three practical measures would improve performance further: local recalibration of the decision threshold against the population and camera in use, adoption of vision-threatening DR rather than any DR as the primary screening endpoint, and integration of OCT or clinical risk variables such as HbA1c and albuminuria into a multimodal decision rule, an approach that has recently been proposed on the basis of similar observations []. It is also worth noting that the benefit of autonomous AI may lie as much in improving screening uptake as in diagnostic accuracy: a randomized trial in youth with diabetes showed that autonomous AI at the point of care substantially increased eye examination completion rates [].
 4.7. Strengths and Limitations
 The strengths of this study include its prospective design, consecutive enrollment in an unselected real-world pathway, masking of the reference standard to the index test result, parallel reporting at the eye and patient levels, the use of OCT rather than photographic surrogates as the reference standard for macular edema, the inclusion of patients with prediabetes, and an analysis of the clinical determinants of discordance that goes beyond the reporting of summary accuracy statistics.
 Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study evaluating a single AI system at a single software version; the findings should not be extrapolated to other algorithms, cameras or populations, particularly given the wide between-system and between-camera variability documented in head-to-head comparisons [,]. Second, the reference standard was a clinical ophthalmological examination rather than reading-center grading of ETDRS seven-field stereoscopic photographs, and discordant cases were not adjudicated by a third method, so misclassification by the reference standard cannot be excluded []. Third, the number of eyes with severe disease was small (8 eyes graded 2 by the reference standard), which precluded precise estimation of accuracy for vision-threatening DR, the outcome of greatest clinical importance []. Fourth, image quality and the frequency of ungradable studies were not systematically recorded, although technical failure rate is an important determinant of real-world screening performance and has been shown in large program evaluations to influence both throughput and accuracy [,,,,]. Fifth, the achieved sample size was below the protocol target of 500, and subgroup and discordance analyses were not adjusted for multiplicity and are therefore exploratory. Sixth, visual acuity, intraocular pressure, refraction, longitudinal outcomes and cost data were not collected, so the clinical and economic consequences of the observed misclassification could not be quantified. Finally, eye-level analyses treat fellow eyes as independent units; although a patient-clustered bootstrap confirmed that this had little effect on our conclusions, eye-level confidence intervals should still be interpreted with this caveat in mind.
 5. Conclusions
 In a real-world internal medicine screening pathway, an AI-assisted retinal screening system detected DR with high negative predictive value but systematically over-called disease, achieving only fair-to-moderate agreement with masked ophthalmological examination and falling short of established screening benchmarks for sensitivity and specificity. Performance was poorest in patients with prediabetes and for OCT-defined macular edema, and the algorithm’s positive calls were not significantly associated with macular thickness. Discordance was not random: false-positive classification was associated with a worse metabolic and microvascular profile, particularly albuminuria, whereas missed cases had milder disease. These findings support the deployment of such systems as a rule-out triage adjunct with ophthalmological confirmation of all positive results, rather than as an autonomous replacement for specialist examination, and underline the importance of local validation and recalibration before clinical implementation.
